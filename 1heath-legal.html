In Short
  • No single licence lets 1Heath operate. About a dozen laws and bodies touch it. Patient data, the safety checks, joining the national health network (ABDM) and the prescription itself matter most.
  • Is it a medical device? CDSCO's 2026 guidance leaves out software that only stores and passes on records[1]. 1Heath's warnings about allergies, doses and repeated medicines inform a doctor's decision, so they are likely to be a medical device. Our reading is Class A or B, but the class depends on the intended use we state and the risk of each output, and is not settled: dose and allergy warnings could be placed higher. For Class A software that is not sterile and does not measure, the guidance asks for registration, not a licence; Class B means a state licence after an audit, in about 4 to 5 months[2]. We will send CDSCO the exact intended-use statement and workflow and ask for a written answer before any real patient, and the budget below must be redone if the answer is a higher class.
  • Patient data: the DPDP Rules were notified on 13 November 2025. Most duties and all penalties start on 13 May 2027[3]; penalties go up to ₹250 crore[4]. Until then the older 2011 rules on sensitive data under the IT Act still apply, and they already cover medical records[5]. 1Heath will comply with both before its first real patient, not in 2027.
  • Cost: the first year of licences, audits, certification, insurance and legal opinions costs about ₹4.7 to 11.9 lakh (our estimate from sourced prices), not counting staff time.
  • Complaints: a patient can complain to the doctor's medical council, to a consumer commission, or to 1Heath and then the Data Protection Board. In negligence cases, poor records lose cases: in 253 national consumer commission cases, deficient records were the second most common failing[6].

Is 1Heath a Medical Device?

CDSCO, India's medical device regulator, issued its guidance on medical device software in July 2026, after a draft in October 2025[29][30]. It adds no new law; it explains how the Medical Devices Rules 2017 apply to software. What decides the matter is the software's intended purpose, function by function.

Not a medical device, according to the guidance: hospital and clinic information systems that manage patient data, such as admission, scheduling, billing, clinical communication and storing records; software that only transfers, stores, converts or searches data; general communication tools; and software for teaching or general wellness. Such software becomes a medical device if a medical function is added to it[1].

How the rest is classed: two questions decide the class. How serious is the patient's condition, and how much does the software's output weigh in the decision?

Duties in either class, from the guidance[1]:

  • A quality management system, with records of design, risk management, testing and every software version.
  • Cybersecurity: encryption, access control, audit trails and protection of patient data.
  • Documented procedures for updates, retraining and version control. For AI, an "algorithm change protocol" may be agreed in advance where it fits. Every software change must be notified, and major changes need approval.
  • After launch: complaint handling and monitoring for drift. Serious unexpected adverse events and recalls must be reported to the regulator within 15 days, and an unsafe product must be reported immediately.

Our plan: ask CDSCO for a written classification of each function in months 1 to 6, and build the quality system as if the checks were Class B from day one. That costs little extra, because version control, testing records and audit trails are already part of how 1Heath is built.

An open question on timing. If CDSCO says the checks are a device, our reading is that they cannot be offered to paying clinics until the registration or licence is held. Whether the pilot runs with the checks switched off until then is a decision still to be made, and a question for our lawyer.

Our Draft Intended-Use Statement

"1Heath is software for registered doctors in outpatient clinics. It records the consultation with the patient's consent, drafts the note and prescription from the doctor's own words for the doctor to review and sign, and shows warnings about recorded allergies, repeated medicines and unusual doses to inform the doctor's decision. It does not diagnose, does not choose treatment and does not advise patients." This is our draft; CDSCO's guidance makes the intended use decide the class[1], so we will ask CDSCO in writing whether the warnings are a device, and which class.

Until CDSCO answers: the first pilot is free to clinics, runs with the safety checks switched on under ethics committee approval, and charges nothing. No paying clinic gets the checks before CDSCO's written answer and any registration it requires (founder decision, 23 November 2026).

Patient Data: The DPDP Act and Its Rules

  1. 13 Nov 2025Rules notifiedThe Data Protection Board exists in law[31]
  2. 13 Nov 2026Consent managersRegistration of consent managers begins[3]
  3. 13 May 2027Main duties and penaltiesNotice, consent, security, breach reporting, erasure and rights; penalties apply[3]

Where things stand on 21 November 2026:

  • Until 13 May 2027 the older rules still apply. Section 43A of the IT Act and its 2011 rules treat medical records as sensitive data. They require consent in writing or electronically, a published privacy policy, a grievance officer who resolves complaints within one month, and reasonable security; ISO 27001 is one recognised standard. The DPDP Act removes section 43A on 13 May 2027[5].
  • In January 2026 MeitY proposed bringing the deadline forward to November 2026, but no change had been notified by late October 2026[3][32].
  • No chairperson or member of the Data Protection Board had been appointed as of 1 November 2026[32].
  • The law is settled; enforcement is not yet working.

Who Is Responsible for What

  • The clinic or doctor decides why a patient's data is collected, so on our reading they are the "data fiduciary" for clinic records. 1Heath processes those records for them under a written contract. One caution: if 1Heath decides how records move between clinics in the shared record, it may be a fiduciary for that too. This is a question for our lawyer.
  • The patient's own copy in the Patient app, and any research on consented data, make 1Heath a data fiduciary in its own right.
  • Speech and AI vendors process data for 1Heath and need contracts that set retention to zero, turn training off, and keep data in India.
  • Children: clinics and doctors providing health services to a child are exempt from the separate parental-consent process, but only as far as needed to protect the child's health[33]. 1Heath's own Patient app is not exempt, so a parent manages a child's account.

The Duties, and the Penalties Behind Them

  • A clear notice and specific consent before recording; withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it.
  • Reasonable security safeguards. Logs are kept for at least one year[31].
  • A breach is reported to every affected person and to the Board, with a detailed report within 72 hours[34]. Cyber incidents also go to CERT-In within six hours, and system logs are kept for 180 days in India[9].
  • Requests from patients (access, correction, erasure where the law allows) are answered within 90 days[31]. 1Heath will aim for 7 days.

Records That Carry Extra Rules

Some records have their own confidentiality laws on top of the data law. On our reading, to be confirmed by a lawyer:

  • Mental health: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 gives a right to confidentiality of mental health records.
  • HIV: the HIV and AIDS Act 2017 bars disclosing a person's HIV status without informed consent, except in narrow cases.
  • Pregnancy: the MTP Act bars revealing the name of a woman whose pregnancy was terminated, and the PC-PNDT Act bars telling anyone the sex of a fetus. 1Heath has no feature that records or shows fetal sex.
  • Narcotic medicines: the NDPS rules need special forms and registers for essential narcotic drugs, so these orders need their own design before 1Heath handles them.

Joining ABDM: Steps, Tests and Cost

ABDM is the national system that links a patient's records across clinics through the ABHA number. It has about 98.9 crore ABHA accounts and 127.5 crore linked records (5 November 2026)[36]. For a private clinic, joining is voluntary. For 1Heath it is how a record reaches the next doctor, so we will join.

  • Security audit: one security firm that offers these audits quotes ₹80,000 to ₹2.5 lakh per audit, depending on scope[12]. With a web app and an Android app, budget ₹1.6 to 5 lakh.
  • Functional test: paid to the empanelled agency; we found no published price, so it must be quoted.
  • Incentives: the Digital Health Incentive Scheme pays for records linked to ABDM. By 18 June 2026 it had paid over ₹26 crore to software companies and over ₹107 crore to hospitals[37]. We have not counted any of it in our plans.
  • Competition: ABDM also connects 1Heath's competitors, including the government's own clinic software. Joining is necessary, not an advantage.

Prescriptions and Signatures

The rules a prescription must follow are already built into the 1Heath format:

  • the doctor's registration number;
  • generic names written legibly;
  • the patient's name, age and address;
  • the dose and total quantity;
  • no remote prescribing of medicines the telemedicine rules bar[13][14].

The 2002 ethics regulations are the ones in force. The 2023 NMC code, which would have made generic-only prescribing compulsory, is still on hold[38].

The signature is the open question. A "slide to sign" proves who pressed the button inside 1Heath. The two kinds of electronic signature that the IT Act clearly recognises cost money:

Aadhaar eSign on every prescription

  1. Price per signed document[15]₹5

  3. Cost a month, per doctor₹3,750

A signing certificate for each doctor

  1. Two-year individual signing certificate[16]₹2,100 plus GST
  2. With 18% GST, spread over 24 months₹2,478
  3. Cost a month, per doctorabout ₹103

A signature on every prescription at ₹5 would cost more than the doctor's whole subscription. A per-doctor certificate is cheap, but it usually comes on a USB token, which does not fit a phone; cloud-held certificates need checking. On 13 October 2026 the NMC said doctors may sign electronic clinical records remotely with recognised digital or electronic signatures, provided the doctor reviews the record first, keeps the credentials personal, and the facility keeps audit trails and time stamps. That advisory names reports and discharge summaries, not prescriptions[39]. The plan:

  • the doctor signs in securely, and the slide to sign is recorded in an audit trail;
  • a recognised signature is applied where the law requires it.

For remote consultations, the telemedicine guidelines already accept a photo, scan or digital copy of a signed prescription, or an e-prescription, sent by any messaging platform[14]. Which in-clinic prescriptions need a recognised signature is the first question for our lawyer.

Company, Tax and Brand

  • Company: a private limited company, so investors can hold shares. Professional fees run about ₹5,000 to 15,000, and stamp duty varies by state; the whole process takes about three weeks[27].
  • Start-up recognition: DPIIT recognition and Udyam registration bring tax and trademark benefits.
  • GST: software subscriptions carry 18% GST. Registration is required above ₹20 lakh of turnover[40], or earlier where the law requires it. Marketplace sales attract the platform taxes covered on the supply page.
  • Brand: register "1Heath" in software, medical services and the related classes. The official fee is ₹4,500 a class for a start-up, and registration takes about 9 to 12 months[28].
  • Insurance: cyber insurance with ₹1 crore of cover costs about ₹18,000 to 35,000 a year for a firm with turnover up to ₹1 crore. Healthcare firms pay 30% to 50% more, which makes it about ₹23,400 to 52,500[35]. Professional liability cover for software errors is to be quoted.

Who Is Liable When the AI Is Wrong?

  • No Indian law yet assigns liability for AI errors. The national AI guidelines of November 2025 are voluntary and say existing laws apply. They use a wrong AI medical diagnosis as their own example of an unclear case: is the developer, the hospital or the doctor responsible[24]?
  • ICMR's 2023 guidelines list accountability and liability among their core principles, because an AI cannot itself be held to account[23].
  • In practice, the doctor who signs remains responsible for the prescription. That is why 1Heath never signs, never sends anything to a patient unsigned, and shows the doctor exactly what it changed.
  • 1Heath is responsible for its software working as described. Its contract with each clinic will say so plainly and will not hide behind a blanket disclaimer.
Why records matter in a dispute. A review of 253 medical negligence cases decided by the national consumer commission from 2015 to 2019 found negligence in 135 (53%). Deficient medical records were the second most common failing, in 22% of the errors found. Some doctors were held liable for poor records even where they were not negligent. The commission's words: "poor records mean poor defence, no records mean no defence"[6]. A complete, signed, time-stamped record is protection for the doctor, not only a convenience. To be accepted in court, an electronic record also needs a certificate under section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 (our reading), so 1Heath must be able to support one for any record.

The Ground Reality

  • Health data is a target. The 2022 ransomware attack on AIIMS Delhi put the data of about 4 crore patients at risk, and a 2023 leak linked to ICMR allegedly exposed details of 81.5 crore Indians[44]. In January to August 2026, healthcare was the third most-named sector on ransomware leak sites in India (20 entries), though a listing alone does not prove a breach[45].
  • Rules arrive slowly, then all at once. The e-pharmacy rules have been in draft since August 2018 and before the courts since[19]. The 2023 medical code is on hold[38]. The data protection board has no members yet[32]. 1Heath builds to the strictest likely version, so a new rule does not force a rebuild.
  • Advertising is restricted. The 1954 Drugs and Magic Remedies Act bars advertising medicines for listed diseases, including diabetes, heart disease and obesity[20]. The Patient app will never promote a medicine, brand or seller.
  • Most problems will surface at the counter, not in a court. We expect a wrong medicine name or a missing record to be noticed first by the clinic or the patient (our assumption). A fast, human answer from 1Heath's support desk is the first line; we will publish how quickly we answer.

The Order We Will Do It In

Questions for the Lawyer and for CDSCO

The full brief for the first lawyer meeting is this list plus three more: compliance in the product, launch and medicine supply. Tax questions for the chartered accountant are on the finance page.

  1. Which risk class does each 1Heath safety check fall into, and is the prescription draft outside the device rules if it adds nothing the doctor did not say?
  2. Does a prescription signed by an authenticated slide, with an audit trail, satisfy the Drugs Rules and the IT Act? If not, which prescriptions need eSign or a certificate?
  3. For clinic records, is 1Heath a processor for the clinic, even for the shared record across clinics? For the Patient app, a data fiduciary? Which contract terms follow?
  4. Until 13 May 2027, what form of consent satisfies the 2011 sensitive data rules for clinic records and recordings?
  5. Is 1Heath an intermediary under the IT Rules for uploaded reports or medicine orders?
  6. Do the e-commerce rules apply if clinics order medicines through 1Heath from licensed stockists, and may 1Heath, as a licensed distributor, sell to a pharmacy owned by the prescribing doctor on its published price list under the medical ethics code? While 1Heath also distributes medicines, is a free or low-priced Doctor app a benefit in kind under the marketing code, which binds distributors too[46]?
  7. Can 1Heath record consultations in the pilot as service improvement, or is it research that needs ethics committee approval?
  8. What must the clinic contract say about liability, and what insurance should 1Heath and the clinic each hold?
