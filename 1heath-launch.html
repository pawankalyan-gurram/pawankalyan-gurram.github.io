  • The legal map was not the whole launch. Ten more areas decide whether 1Heath can actually reach a clinic: app store rules, messages to patients, where data lives, payments, hiring, raising money, ethics approval, accessibility, who we let on, and the documents we must write.
  • The app store forces an early decision. Google Play requires health apps to come from an organisation account. App Stores: Health Apps Have Extra Rules

  • Account: Google Play requires health apps, such as medical apps, to be published from an organisation account. That needs a D-U-N-S number, which Dun & Bradstreet issues free[2]. The account costs a one-time US$25[7]. So the company must exist before the Play listing does.
  • Declaration: every developer must complete Play's Health apps declaration[1].
  • The device question: an app regulated as a medical device must be declared as one and labelled "Medical Device", with proof of approval on request. Every other health app must state in its description that it is not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any condition[1].
    • If CDSCO classes 1Heath's safety checks as a device (see the legal page), the listing must say so and we must hold the registration.
    • If it does not, the disclaimer applies.
    • Either way, the answer is needed before the listing.
  • Privacy policy: a public web page, not a PDF, linked in Play Console and inside the app[1].
  • The demo: the Android demo will be shared as a download link for testing with fictional patients only. Real clinics get 1Heath through Play.

Messages to Patients: SMS and WhatsApp

  • SMS: every business SMS in India, including one-time passwords, goes through the telecom operators' registration system (DLT). The company registers itself, its sender name, its consent templates and the exact text of each message, and only Indian entities can register[11]. Registration costs ₹5,000 plus ₹900 GST, so ₹5,900[6].
  • WhatsApp: Meta charges per message. A prescription link is a "utility" message, at ₹0.115 plus 18% GST. Since 1 October 2026, utility messages inside the 24-hour reply window are charged too, and replies beyond 1,000 free service messages a month per number are charged[12]. Marketing messages cost ₹0.8631[4], and 1Heath sends none.

WhatsApp cost for one doctor, a month

  1. Visits a month, each sending one prescription linkour assumption: about 30 a day, as on the business page750

That is real money against the price we charge a doctor. The secure link page does the same job when the patient's number is not on WhatsApp, and SMS stays for one-time passwords only.

Where Data May Live

  • The DPDP Act does not force health data to stay in India. It lets the government block transfers to particular countries. No country had been restricted on the public record by 31 August 2026, and that power starts on 13 May 2027[13].
  • Stricter sector rules still win. RBI's 2018 rule keeps payment data in India[13]; our payment provider holds it.
  • ABDM keeps no records centrally. The National Health Authority stores no patient records. Records stay with the clinic's provider, here 1Heath on the clinic's behalf, and are shared only with the patient's consent[14].
  • Our choice: servers, backups and the speech and drafting models all run in India. That costs nothing extra today, and it is the answer clinics and patients expect.

Getting Paid

  • Recurring payments: under RBI's e-mandate framework of April 2026, a subscription up to ₹15,000 can renew without an OTP once the customer has approved the mandate. The bank must give 24 hours' notice before each debit, and the customer can pause or cancel at any time[15]. A clinic subscription is far below the limit.
  • Gateway fee: a standard payment-gateway plan charges 2% a transaction plus 18% GST[5], so ₹23.60 on a ₹1,000 payment. On a percentage fee, monthly and annual billing cost the same; annual billing only cuts failed renewals.
  • Invoices: 18% GST on the subscription; see the legal page.

Hiring: Labour Codes, PF, ESI and POSH

We will register for PF and ESI voluntarily from the first hire rather than waiting for the thresholds. It is cheaper to start right than to fix records later, and it helps hiring.

Raising Money and Selling to Government

  • Angel tax is gone. The tax on start-ups raising money above fair value was abolished for all investors from the 2025-26 financial year[18].
  • DPIIT recognition exempts a start-up from earnest money and from prior-turnover and experience rules in central government tenders. On GeM, the government marketplace, the Startup Runway programme does the same[19]. This matters later for government clinics.
  • Insurance claims are not a near-term channel. The National Health Claims Exchange had only about 34 insurers and TPAs and 300 hospitals by mid-2024, and adoption is still slow[20]. Small clinics rarely handle cashless claims, so we leave it out of plans.
  • Foreign investment rules for a health software company are a question for our lawyer before taking money from outside India.

Ethics Approval Before Recording Real Patients

Our plan to record 50 real consultations and measure errors is research on people and their health data. The way we will do it:

  1. Step 1ProtocolPurpose, consent forms in the patient's language, how audio is stored and deleted, who sees it
  2. Step 2Ethics committeeApproval from a registered institutional ethics committee; fee to be quoted
  3. Step 3RegistryRegister on the Clinical Trials Registry of India before the first patient; free, approval uploaded[3]
  4. Step 4Record and reportWritten consent from patient and doctor; results published, good or bad

The registry lists observational studies as well as trials, and its advice is "when in doubt, register"[3]. Doing it properly also makes the results credible to doctors, investors and CDSCO.

Accessibility

  • In November 2024 the Supreme Court held that accessibility rules under the disability law must be mandatory, not advisory[21].
  • Draft rules gazetted on 20 July 2026 would cover websites and apps offered in India. Each firm would publish an Accessibility Conformance Report, and smaller firms would get 18 months to comply[22].
  • 1Heath's Patient app serves older patients and people with poor eyesight, so we design for access from the start:
    • large text that still works at double size (this site is tested that way);
    • screen-reader labels;
    • no meaning carried by colour alone;
    • voice in the patient's language.

Who We Let On

  • Doctors: each one's registration is checked against the medical council or ABDM's professional registry before the account can sign a prescription. Unqualified practitioners are refused, even though in the rural districts one study surveyed, about 7 in 10 people went to them first[23].
  • Systems of medicine: an Ayurveda or homeopathy doctor is registered with their own council. 1Heath warns when a prescription falls outside the doctor's system, as the systems page explains.
  • Clinics: Telangana applies the Clinical Establishments Act 2010, under which clinics, including on our reading single-doctor clinics, register with the district authority[24]. We will ask for the clinic's registration at sign-up.
  • Pharmacies and suppliers: drug licences are checked before any order flows, as the supply page sets out.
  • Staff: every action leaves a trace of who did what, for what reason and when, as the security page describes.

Questions to Add for the Lawyer

  1. Can 1Heath ship a Play build with the safety checks switched off, truthfully declared as not a medical device, before CDSCO answers, or must it wait?
  2. Is the 50-consultation accuracy study research needing ethics approval, or service evaluation? We plan to treat it as research either way.
  3. What foreign-investment conditions apply to a health software company?
  4. Does sending a prescription link by WhatsApp need anything beyond the patient's consent and Meta's templates?
