Compliance Made Simple: Every User, Every Feature

In Short
  • Our rule: the product carries the compliance, not the doctor. Every legal duty is met by a default, an automatic record, or one tap, and never by a form the doctor fills in.
  • The law already offers lawful ways to keep things simple. On our reading, the clinic takes one consent and 1Heath works under contract as its processor[1]. The notice can be in the patient's own language[2]. A medical emergency needs no consent[3]. Clinics treating children skip the separate parental-identity check[4]. Remote prescriptions can be a scan or digital copy of a signed one[5]. Most of these DPDP provisions apply from 13 May 2027; until then the 2011 sensitive data rules still ask for consent to collect health data[6].
  • Some apparent loopholes are traps. Pre-ticked or bundled consent, reusing treatment data to train models without asking, storing Aadhaar numbers, calling the software "not a medical device" just to skip CDSCO, and referral fees all look like shortcuts. Each one is a legal risk, and 1Heath uses none of them.
  • Consent takes about ten seconds. The desk plays the notice in the patient's language, and the patient taps or says yes. Research use is a separate "yes", off by default. Withdrawing takes one tap.
  • Our review corrected four statements on other pages: the patient's address on prescriptions, GST credit for clinics with a counter, WhatsApp's charges since October 2026, and the start-up turnover limit as amended. See the last section.

The Rule: Compliance Lives in the Product

  • Defaults do the work. The safe, lawful choice is already selected; nobody has to remember a rule.
  • One action per legal need. One tap for consent, one slide to sign, nothing more.
  • The patient's language. Notices are spoken or shown in the language the patient chooses.
  • Never block care. A missing consent stops the recording, never the consultation.

Every action is logged automatically: who did what, why and when. Proof of compliance is a by-product of using 1Heath, not extra work.

Lawful Simplifications We Use

These are provisions the law itself offers. "Our reading" marks the ones a lawyer must confirm before we rely on them.

Timing. The DPDP provisions below (sections 5 to 8 and 14, and the Rules) take effect on 13 May 2027. Until then, section 43A of the IT Act and its 2011 rules apply: medical records are sensitive data, and collecting them needs consent in writing or by electronic means[6]. So before May 2027 the "legitimate use" backstop is not available, and the clinic's consent covers the record as well as the recording.

Consent in Ten Seconds

Special Cases

  • A child: the parent or guardian answers at the clinic; no identity check is needed there[4]. At 18, the young adult is asked again.
  • A patient who cannot read: the spoken notice and a spoken yes; the desk confirms by voice.
  • A person with a disability who cannot decide: a guardian appointed by a court or a designated authority gives consent[20]. Rare; the desk flags it.
  • An emergency: treatment and records first, consent later[3].
  • A family member speaking for an adult patient: the patient's own yes is still needed, unless the patient is incapacitated and has named that person as nominee[9].

When Two Rules Collide

  • Erasure versus record-keeping. The law on keeping records wins[1]. The patient's account closes, but the clinic keeps its record.
  • Privacy versus the next doctor's need. The patient decides who sees the history; in an emergency it opens, and the patient is told[3].
  • A doctor leaves the clinic. The records belong to the clinic, which is the data fiduciary on our reading, not to the departing doctor.
  • A clinic leaves 1Heath. It gets a full export in an open format, and 1Heath deletes its copy after the agreed period. Records are never held hostage.

Questions for the Lawyer

  1. Can the clinic rely on "legitimate use" for the basic record, keeping consent for recording and research only?
  2. Is 1Heath a processor for clinic records, and what must the processor contract contain?
  3. Is a logged spoken "yes" valid consent, and how long must the recording of it be kept?
  4. Can an in-clinic prescription be signed by secure sign-in plus slide to sign, like the copy the telemedicine guidelines accept for remote care?
  5. How should a pharmacist's Schedule H1 register be kept electronically?
  6. Until 13 May 2027, does a spoken or tapped yes meet the 2011 rules' consent "in writing" for health data?
  7. For outpatient records, what retention period does the law require, so that an erasure request can be refused?
