Doctor, Clinic, Hospital, Patient: 1Heath Through Each One's Eyes

What goes wrong today for each of the four, what patients, doctors and chemists say in polls and forums, refills on time, the value of the record without selling it, and how we decide when interests clash.

In Short
  • Four people, four different problems. The doctor has two minutes and no history. The clinic owner earns from the pharmacy counter but loses money to expiry, stock-outs and middlemen. The hospital has software that does not talk to anyone else. The patient carries paper, repeats tests and pays from their own pocket.
  • One product has to work for all four, and each must get something on the first day, or they will not use it.
  • Who pays, and when: doctors pay a membership under ₹500 a month from the start; licensed clinic pharmacies pay through the supply they already buy, open to all at one price list; hospitals pay per facility later; patients get the record free and may later pay for delivery or a care membership. Safety features are never paid extras.
  • What people say: polls and patient forums show that families buy from local chemists, distrust commissions, waste medicines and skip doses, and that chemists are fighting online sellers; details below, with a refill service built on the prescription.
  • Where the views clash, we chose the patient's safety and freedom of choice, and say so below.

The Doctor

What Goes Wrong Today

  • No time. Studies in India put the average primary-care consultation at 1.5 to 2.3 minutes[1]. There is no time to ask about every medicine the patient takes elsewhere.
  • No history. Patients arrive with loose slips, or nothing. What another doctor, an Ayurvedic practitioner or a chemist gave them is usually unknown (see the problem).
  • Software that costs time. Typing into a computer takes the doctor's eyes off the patient. Most small clinics have no computer at all.
  • No wish to pay for software. Doctors we spoke to said plainly they would not pay ₹1,500 a month for software that helps record patient data. They would pay a small fee that also saves their clinic money.

What 1Heath Does

  • Listens to the consultation in the doctor's own language and writes the note and prescription, which the doctor checks and signs.
  • Shows the patient's history from earlier visits, if the patient agrees.
  • Checks allergies, pregnancy, children's doses and clashing medicines before the doctor signs.
  • Never picks or ranks a medicine by what anyone earns. The doctor chooses.

What the doctor pays: one membership under ₹500 a month, which also gives the clinic pharmacy cheaper buying (worked example).

The Clinic Owner and the Pharmacy Counter

What Goes Wrong Today

  • The money is in the counter. A small clinic earns from its own pharmacy as much as from consultations: patients buy there what the doctor prescribes. On medicines that are not price-controlled, chemists keep a median of about 28% of the price[2].
  • Buying is scattered. Big brands come only through their authorised stockists[3]; small makers' brands come through other dealers and agents. Each takes a share.
  • Waste. Medicines expire on the shelf, popular ones run out, and a recalled batch is hard to trace back to the patients who got it.
  • Credit and paperwork. Stockists give credit and expect payment; GST invoices, batch numbers and expiry dates are tracked by hand.

What 1Heath Does

  • The Pharmacy app shows what was prescribed, tracks stock by batch and expiry, and flags what will run out.
  • Ordering through 1Heath at a lower buying price: first through a licensed partner stockist, later from 1Heath's own licensed stock of small and mid-size makers' medicines (supply page).
  • Any batch on the government's not-of-standard-quality list is blocked in every clinic at once.

What the clinic pays: nothing for the Pharmacy app. Any licensed pharmacy may order through 1Heath on one published price list, whether or not its doctor is a member. 1Heath earns a fee per order or a trade margin on the supply, never a payment to the doctor (how 1Heath earns).

The Hospital

What Goes Wrong Today

  • Records stay inside. About 35% of Indian hospitals use electronic records, mostly large city chains[4], and what they hold rarely follows the patient to the next doctor.
  • Doctors type. Hospital software is built for billing and administration, so doctors end up as data-entry staff.
  • Insurance paperwork. Ayushman Bharat alone has 38,466 empanelled hospitals and has paid for 12.69 crore admissions[5]; each claim needs clean records.
  • Government hospitals are stretched. In Telangana, 29 of 33 districts have no government MRI scanner[6], and outpatient queues are long.

What 1Heath Does, Later

  • The scribe and safety checks on top of the hospital's existing software, on web and desktop.
  • Records that follow the patient out, through the national health record system (ABDM), whose incentive scheme had paid hospitals and software companies more than ₹130 crore by June 2026[7]; whether it continues is not yet announced.
  • For government hospitals, the same open standards, offered through state health programmes.

What a hospital pays: a price per facility, set after the clinic pilot. Not counted in today's business numbers. Many hospital doctors also run evening clinics, which is where they meet 1Heath first.

The Patient and Family

What Goes Wrong Today

  • Families pay most of the bill. Spending from people's own pockets was 42.7% of all health spending in 2023–24; medicines alone were about a fifth of day-to-day health spending[8].
  • Paper and repetition. Slips get lost, tests are repeated, and the next doctor starts from nothing.
  • Help between visits is missing. Nobody reminds a patient with diabetes or high blood pressure about refills, food, tests or the next visit.

What 1Heath Gives Free, Always

  • The patient's own record and every signed prescription, readable on their phone, in their language.
  • Family access that the patient chooses and can withdraw.
  • Every safety check. Safety is never a paid feature, and patient data is never sold.

Why no pharmacy is chosen in advance. The clinic's own counter is where many doctors earn, and the patient's cheapest option may be elsewhere. So the patient sees every licensed option side by side and picks each time.

Busy doctors care more about time saved per patient.

Refills on Time: Turning the Prescription into Steady Care

Medicines for long-term illness, led by blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol, are about 40% of India's medicine sales[34]. One online pharmacy built mainly on such patients and cheaper equivalents sold ₹510 crore in 2024–25[35]. 1Heath has an advantage none of them has: the signed prescription says which medicine, how much and for how long.

  1. 1The doctor signsMedicine, dose and duration, and whether it may be repeated
  2. 2A few days before it runs outThe patient gets a reminder, if they agreed to reminders
  3. 3The patient picks a pharmacyClinic counter, nearby chemist or online, with prices side by side
  4. 4Delivered or ready to collectExactly the quantity prescribed
  • Safety rules: refills only within the duration and repeats the doctor wrote; never for antibiotics, habit-forming or other specially controlled medicines; the pharmacist checks the prescription every time.
  • How 1Heath earns: a small flat fee per refill from the pharmacy that fills it, the same for every pharmacy and brand, never shared with the doctor. Online pharmacies pay little for repeat customers[9], so local chemists that gain steady customers are the likelier payers (to be tested).
  • Consent: reminders and offers only with the patient's separate yes, which they can withdraw at any time.

The Value of the Record, Without Selling It
