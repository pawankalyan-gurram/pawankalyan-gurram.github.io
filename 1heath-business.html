  • How small clinics earn: many run their own pharmacy counter, where patients buy what the doctor prescribes, and the clinic keeps the gap between its buying price and the printed price.   • What 1Heath offers: a membership of ₹199 to ₹299 a month (final price after the pilot) for doctors whose clinic has its own pharmacy. What the doctor pays for is cheaper buying: the clinic pharmacy gets medicines, surgical items, injections and IV fluids through 1Heath for less than it pays today. How a Small Clinic Earns, and Where 1Heath Fits

Many small clinics have their own pharmacy counter: the doctor prescribes and the patient buys there. In a study of rural primary care, 82% of private practices dispensed medicines in the clinic, and their fees often included the medicines[1]. The clinic's income is then the consultation fee plus the counter's margin; doctors we spoke to say the counter is often the larger part, but no national survey has measured it yet. On price-controlled medicines the ceiling fixes the margin: every brand of paracetamol 650 mg is capped at ₹2.04 a tablet before GST[7]. On the rest, about 82% of sales, the maker sets the price, and trade generics leave the widest gaps[2][8].

What a clinic pharmacy saves, founder's case (our assumptions)

  1. The clinic pharmacy buys in a month, from everyone₹1,50,000
  2. It orders 6 in 10 rupees of that through 1Heath₹90,000
  3. At a buying price 5% lower than today, it keeps more each month₹4,500

The same, cautious case

  1. The clinic pharmacy buys in a month₹1,00,000
  2. It orders half of that through 1Heath₹50,000
  3. At a 5% lower buying price, it keeps more₹2,500

Doctors told us they would not pay for software alone: it mostly helps the patient, and the doctor gains only indirectly, by treating better. When the same software came with cheaper buying for their clinic pharmacy, they said they would pay a monthly membership. So the membership is sold for the medicine savings, and the software comes with it. The fee is flat: it does not change with how much the clinic buys or with what the doctor prescribes, and the Doctor app never shows prices, brands or margins.

The legal line. A licensed clinic pharmacy that buys cheaper from a licensed wholesaler, sells at or below the printed price and keeps the larger margin is ordinary trade, with the discount on the invoice to the pharmacy business. What the law forbids is any commission, cash, gift, trip or other benefit to a doctor personally for prescribing, whether from a maker or from a distributor[9][10]. The membership sits close to that line, and we say so plainly. It is sold for cheaper buying, and when the doctor owns the clinic pharmacy, the doctor gains from those lower prices; outside reviewers warn that this, with software included below its cost, could be read as a benefit to a prescriber. Our safeguards: the fee is flat and never linked to purchases or prescriptions; discounts go on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business; member and non-member prices are published; the prescribing screen never shows prices, brands or margins; and the AI never chooses or ranks a medicine by margin. Whether this is lawful is the first question for the lawyer, before the first sale. If the answer is no, the model changes: for example, the membership is offered only to the pharmacy business, or the software is priced on its own.

What these numbers say: the AI cost decides the outcome as much as the supply margin. At today's measured ₹3.09 a visit, even the founder's case loses about ₹75 crore over five years and is still losing ₹30 crore in year 5, so cutting the cost per visit to well under ₹1 is a condition, not an improvement. With the planned fall in AI cost, in the founder's case the business needs about ₹25 crore of outside money to reach profit in year 5, plus ₹72 to ₹145 crore for stock once it holds its own. Banks lend against stock only to businesses with profits or security, so early stock money is equity or the partner stockists' balance sheet. In the cautious case the business is still losing money in year 5. It closes only with a supply margin of 16% or more, an AI cost well below ₹0.50 a visit, or far more doctors. Which case is true is what the pilot must show.

The Firewall Between Money and Medicine

A company that earns from medicines and also helps write prescriptions has an obvious conflict. India has seen where that leads: the Supreme Court held that freebies to doctors are prohibited and cannot be deducted as business expenses[10], and a federation of medical representatives told the Court that one company spent ₹1,000 crore on freebies to promote a paracetamol brand[20]. 1Heath's rules:

  • 1

    The AI never chooses by margin. The scribe writes what the doctor says, and nothing it suggests depends on what 1Heath or the pharmacy earns. Prices and margins live only in the Pharmacy app. The clinic's owner, often the doctor, can open it, but the prescribing screen never shows them; the doctor makes the clinical choice on clinical grounds. No own-label medicines will be suggested.

  • 2

    No payment to doctors for prescribing, ordering or referring, in any form.

  • 3

    The patient chooses any pharmacy and any lab; generic names are always shown.

  • 4

    No pharmaceutical sponsorship, no advertising, no sale of data.

  • 5

    Legal opinion first. 1Heath's supply income and the flat lab fee are never paid by or to doctors, but supply grows with what doctors order, so a lawyer must confirm the model before the first sale. Three questions matter most: sales to pharmacies owned by the prescribing doctor; whether a membership that gives a doctor-owned pharmacy lower prices, with the Doctor app included, from a company that also distributes medicines, is a benefit in kind under the marketing code, which binds distributors too[9]; and whether the software and the distributor should be separate companies. Discounts and schemes go on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business, never on the doctor's screens. Doctors may supply medicines to their own patients without a shop licence only within limits[21]; 1Heath will hold and sell medicines only from premises with a wholesale drug licence and a qualified person in charge, and the whole model goes to counsel before the first order.
