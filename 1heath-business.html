Business Model and Revenue, Worked Step by Step

How 1Heath earns, two cases at 1,000 doctors, unit economics, the levers that matter, a five-year path, and the firewall between money and medicine.

In Short
  • Three sources of money: medicine supply to clinic pharmacies, a flat monthly fee from labs, and doctor plans at ₹199 a month or ₹999 for a paid tier. Patients, desks and pharmacies pay nothing.
  • Supply in two stages: first a licensed partner stockist holds the stock and pays 1Heath a fixed fee per order; later, once a city proves about a 14% margin, 1Heath becomes the licensed distributor for small and mid-size makers and keeps a trade margin (how).
  • At 1,000 active doctors the plan case loses about ₹2.65 crore a year through a partner and about ₹2.98 crore holding its own stock. The conservative case loses about ₹5 crore. These replace our earlier figure of a ₹0.45 crore loss, which assumed supply costs of 5.5% and credit from makers; both were too hopeful.
  • What decides it: the AI cost per visit (about ₹13,500 per doctor a year today), the fee or margin on supply, and how much of a clinic's buying moves. Doctor plans matter least.
  • The cash need is large: roughly ₹35 to ₹55 crore of outside money over five years on these assumptions, plus stock if 1Heath holds its own. The first year is a free pilot. Doctors are never paid for prescribing.

Unit Economics

What one active doctor is worth, plan case, stage 1

  1. Revenue per active doctor a year (order fees, lab fees, plan)₹23,684
  2. Minus costs that grow with each doctor (AI ₹13,500, WhatsApp and payments)₹14,197
  3. Left per doctor, per month₹791
  4. If winning one doctor costs about ₹20,000 (our target), it is earned back inabout 25 months

Team and shared costs of ₹3.60 crore are covered at about 3,800 active doctors, before spending on winning new doctors. In stage 2 at an 11% margin, about ₹521 is left per doctor a month and break-even is about 5,760 doctors.

In the conservative case each doctor costs more than they bring in (about −₹1,900 a month): small orders cannot pay for today's AI cost. The AI cost per visit is the single biggest number in this model, and it is the first thing the pilot measures.

Five Years, Plan Case

Doctor numbers are targets, not forecasts, counted at the end of each year. Year 1 is a free pilot with no revenue. Years 2 and 3 run supply through partner stockists; from year 4, 1Heath holds its own stock at a 14% margin and 9% running costs, which is the test for stage 2. Revenue uses the plan case per active doctor on the average number of active doctors in the year. Team and shared costs grow more slowly than doctors (our assumption). Winning doctors costs ₹20,000 each, and 1 in 5 active doctors leaves each year and is replaced at the same cost. AI cost per visit falls from ₹1.50 to ₹0.50 by year 5 (our assumption, not a price anyone has quoted).

How ambitious is 20,000 doctors? India's best-known clinic-app company, Eka Care, founded in 2021, says about 65,000 doctors are onboarded, of whom about 12,000 pay[3]. Treat our year-five figure as a stretch target.

On these assumptions the business needs about ₹35 to ₹55 crore of outside money over five years, more than three times our earlier figure of ₹10.4 crore. If 1Heath holds its own stock, it also needs about ₹50 crore for stock and credit by year 5. A bank lends against stock and bills only once a business shows profits or security (our reading), so early on that money is equity too. Counting stock, staying with partner stockists needs the least cash. The conservative case loses money every year. Which case is true is what the pilot must show.

The Firewall Between Money and Medicine

A company that earns from medicines and also helps write prescriptions has an obvious conflict. India has seen where that leads: the Supreme Court held that freebies to doctors are prohibited and cannot be deducted as business expenses[8], and a federation of medical representatives told the Court that one company spent ₹1,000 crore on freebies to promote a paracetamol brand[9]. 1Heath's rules:

  • 1

    Suggestions never depend on margin. What the doctor is shown never depends on what 1Heath or the pharmacy earns. No own-label medicines will be suggested.

  • 2

    No payment to doctors for prescribing, ordering or referring, in any form.

  • 3

    The patient chooses any pharmacy and any lab; generic names are always shown.

  • 4

    No pharmaceutical sponsorship, no advertising, no sale of data.

  • 5

    Legal opinion first. 1Heath's supply income and the flat lab fee are never paid by or to doctors, but supply grows with what doctors order, so a lawyer must confirm the model before the first sale. Three questions matter most: sales to pharmacies owned by the prescribing doctor; whether a free or low-priced Doctor app, from a company that also distributes medicines, is a benefit in kind under the marketing code, which binds distributors too[10]; and whether the software and the distributor should be separate companies. Discounts and schemes go on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business, never on the doctor's screens. Doctors may supply medicines to their own patients without a shop licence only within limits[11]; 1Heath will hold and sell medicines only from premises with a wholesale drug licence and a qualified person in charge, and the whole model goes to counsel before the first order.
