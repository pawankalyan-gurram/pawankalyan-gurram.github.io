Business Model and Revenue, Worked Step by Step

How 1Heath earns, two cases at 1,000 doctors, unit economics, the levers that matter, a five-year path, and the firewall between money and medicine.

In Short
  • How a small clinic really earns: from its own pharmacy as much as from consultation fees. Patients buy what the doctor prescribes at the clinic's counter, and the clinic keeps the gap between its buying price and the printed price. Chemists keep a median of about 28% of the price[1].
  • What 1Heath offers: the clinic's pharmacy buys medicines, surgical items, injections and IV fluids from 1Heath for less, because 1Heath deals with makers and main distributors directly, with no middlemen and no medical representatives. The clinic keeps more on every strip it sells.
  • What the doctor pays: one membership under ₹500 a month (₹299 in our cases), covering the AI scribe, the patient's history, the safety checks and access to 1Heath supply. Doctors told us they would not pay ₹1,500 for software, but would pay a small fee that saves their pharmacy money.
  • Where the money comes from: the margin on supply. The membership price barely moves the result, so it stays low and every doctor can afford it.
  • The honest numbers (plan case, our assumptions): a loss of about ₹4 to ₹5 crore a year at 1,000 doctors, and about break-even over five years at 20,000 doctors, with ₹38 to ₹82 crore tied up in stock once 1Heath holds its own. The pilot measures every input.

How a Small Clinic Earns, and Where 1Heath Fits

A typical small clinic has its own pharmacy counter. The doctor prescribes and the patient buys there. The clinic's income is the consultation fee plus the pharmacy margin, and doctors we spoke to say the pharmacy margin is often the larger part. That is why clinics choose brands that leave their pharmacy a good margin. On price-controlled medicines the ceiling fixes it: every brand of paracetamol 650 mg is capped at ₹2.04 a tablet before GST[6]. On the rest, about 82% of sales, the maker sets the price, and trade generics leave the widest gaps[1][7].

What 1Heath saves a clinic, plan case (our assumptions)

  1. The clinic pharmacy buys in a month, from everyone₹1,50,000
  2. It orders 6 in 10 rupees of that through 1Heath₹90,000
  3. At a buying price 5% lower than today, it keeps more each month₹4,500
  4. Against a membership of₹299

The same, conservative case

  1. The clinic pharmacy buys in a month₹50,000
  2. It orders 3 in 10 rupees through 1Heath₹15,000
  3. At a 5% lower buying price, it keeps more₹750
  4. Against a membership of₹299

Like a shopping membership that pays for itself in savings, the doctor pays because the pharmacy earns more than the fee. The scribe and the record come at the same price whether or not the clinic buys from 1Heath.

The legal line. A licensed clinic pharmacy that buys cheaper from a licensed wholesaler, sells at or below the printed price and keeps the larger margin is ordinary trade, with the discount on the invoice to the pharmacy business. What the law forbids is any commission, cash, gift or trip to a doctor personally for prescribing, whether from a maker or from a distributor[8][9]. 1Heath stays on the lawful side, and its AI never chooses or ranks a medicine by margin: the doctor chooses.

Five Years, Plan Case

Doctor numbers are targets, counted at the end of each year: one city first, then more cities after a national launch. Year 1 is a free pilot with no revenue. Years 2 and 3 supply through partner stockists; from year 4, 1Heath holds its own stock. Revenue uses the plan case on the average number of doctors in the year, and the cost sheet grows as shown above. AI cost per visit falls from ₹1.50 to ₹0.50 by year 5 (our assumption, not a quoted price).

How ambitious is 20,000 doctors? India's best-known clinic-app company, Eka Care, founded in 2021, says about 65,000 doctors are onboarded, of whom about 12,000 pay[12]. Our year-five figure is a stretch target.

The Firewall Between Money and Medicine

A company that earns from medicines and also helps write prescriptions has an obvious conflict. India has seen where that leads: the Supreme Court held that freebies to doctors are prohibited and cannot be deducted as business expenses[9], and a federation of medical representatives told the Court that one company spent ₹1,000 crore on freebies to promote a paracetamol brand[17]. 1Heath's rules:

  • 1

    The AI never chooses by margin. The scribe writes what the doctor says, and nothing it suggests depends on what 1Heath or the pharmacy earns. Prices and margins live in the Pharmacy app, which the clinic's owner can open; the doctor makes the clinical choice. No own-label medicines will be suggested.

  • 2

    No payment to doctors for prescribing, ordering or referring, in any form.

  • 3

    The patient chooses any pharmacy and any lab; generic names are always shown.

  • 4

    No pharmaceutical sponsorship, no advertising, no sale of data.

  • 5

    Legal opinion first. 1Heath's supply income and the flat lab fee are never paid by or to doctors, but supply grows with what doctors order, so a lawyer must confirm the model before the first sale. Three questions matter most: sales to pharmacies owned by the prescribing doctor; whether a free or low-priced Doctor app, from a company that also distributes medicines, is a benefit in kind under the marketing code, which binds distributors too[8]; and whether the software and the distributor should be separate companies. Discounts and schemes go on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business, never on the doctor's screens. Doctors may supply medicines to their own patients without a shop licence only within limits[18]; 1Heath will hold and sell medicines only from premises with a wholesale drug licence and a qualified person in charge, and the whole model goes to counsel before the first order.
