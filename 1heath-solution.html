The Visit Writes the History

What 1Heath does, how each part answers a root cause, the alternatives we compared it against, and why we chose this design.

In Short
  • 1Heath listens to the consultation, drafts the note and prescription, and checks every line against what was actually said. The check catches lines the doctor did not say or that clash with the record; it cannot judge whether the doctor's own decision is right, which stays with the doctor.
  • The doctor confirms only what is uncertain, then slides to sign.
  • We compared rules alone, a model alone, and both together on 14 unseen simulated consultations in Hindi and Telugu. Only both together was useful and safe[1].
  • Every report sits in one record, so the doctor sees the last result, its date and its lab before ordering the same test again; a repeat needs a medical reason (repeat tests).
  • Nothing is proven on real voices yet; that is the next step.

Design Principles

  • 1

    Zero extra work for the doctor. The doctor talks as usual; the record writes itself.

  • 2

    The doctor decides. Nothing is signed by the software. Anything uncertain is shown for the doctor to confirm.

  • 3

    Nothing invented, nothing guessed. The system never fills in a drug, strength, dose or duration that was not said. Anything it hears but is unsure of is flagged for the doctor, not dropped. A food timing taken from the catalogue is shown in grey as a default the doctor can change. It does not catch everything yet: in simulated tests it captured about 65% of clinical facts[1], so the doctor still reads the note.

  • 4

    Indian reality first. Code-mixed speech, brand-name prescribing, two-minute visits, ceiling fans and corridor noise, AYUSH alongside modern medicine.

  • 5

    One record, many views. Each person in the visit gets their own app on the same consented record.

  • 6

    No side between systems. 1Heath records what patients take from every system and never tells anyone to stop or start one.

How the Record Cuts Repeat Tests

Status: designed; the shared record and lab results are not built. Only the Doctor app demo exists.

The aim is simple: every report a patient has ever had is in one place, the doctor sees the last result before ordering a new one, and a repeat happens only when there is a medical reason. Why tests are repeated today is on the problem page.

What Shared Records Did Elsewhere

No Indian study has measured this yet. In the United States, emergency departments that used a shared record were 59% less likely to repeat a CT scan, 44% less likely to repeat an ultrasound and 67% less likely to repeat a chest X-ray[6]. At one New York emergency department, checking the shared record came with 52% fewer lab tests and 36% fewer scans[7]. These are observational studies in a different health system, so we do not claim the same savings for India.

What Is at Stake for a Family

Even at the government's CGHS rates, among the lowest in the country, an MRI of the brain costs ₹2,500 and a high-resolution CT of the chest ₹2,000[8]. A family on daily wages also loses the day's pay and the fare for every extra trip[9].

What the Pilot Will Measure

  • How often a doctor ordering a test sees an earlier result for it, and how often the earlier result is used instead.
  • Rupees saved per patient on tests not repeated.
  • Any case where a needed test was delayed because an old result was on screen. The target is none; each case is reviewed by the clinical safety lead.

One Visit, Step by Step

How a Line Earns Trust

Every prescription line ends in one of three states. The doctor sees the doctor's own words next to what the system understood.

StateMeaningDoctor's action
VerifiedEvery part matches what was said and the catalogueNothing
ConfirmA name, strength, dose, frequency or duration is uncertain or missingOne tap on a choice; none is pre-selected
Cannot SignConflicts with a recorded allergy, or a banned combinationChange the line

The checks cover allergies, pregnancy, children's doses by weight, duplicates (including medicines the patient already takes), weekly drugs written as daily, banned combinations and look-alike brand names. How each line flows through the system is on the architecture page.

Alternatives We Compared

We did not start with this design. We tested the obvious options on the same unseen simulated consultations[1].

A language model aloneCaptures the most, but adds facts nobody said. One invented allergy or dose is unacceptable
Rules aloneNever invents, but misses most of the conversation and misread who refused what
Global scribesMostly built for English-first clinics, longer visits and insurance billing, not for Indian code-mixed speech, Indian brand names or AYUSH
Indian scribesStrong and already in daily use: HealthPlix runs a scribe on Sarvam speech[10], and in November 2026 the National Health Authority chose EkaCare as technology partner for a voice scribe in public health facilities[11]. Each vendor tests privately, so nobody, including us, can yet show who is most accurate[12]. 1Heath differs in design: every line checked against the words spoken, every system of medicine, and one record after the visit
Model plus rules (1Heath)The model understands the sentence; the rules decide whether each line can be trusted; the doctor resolves the rest

What the Evidence on AI Scribes Says

The best evidence so far is a randomised trial of 238 doctors in 14 specialties in the United States, over about 72,000 visits[13][14]:

  • Time: one scribe cut the time spent writing each note by about 41 seconds, from 4 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes 49 seconds, a modest gain over the doctors without it.
  • Burnout: scores for burnout and workload improved by about 7%.
  • Errors: doctors reported that the notes occasionally had clinically significant mistakes, mostly things left out.

Two lessons shape 1Heath. First, the gain for a doctor who already writes short notes is modest, so the scribe alone is not enough; the value has to come from the shared history it builds. Second, AI notes can leave things out or add things, so every line is checked by rules and signed by the doctor. These results are from American clinics with long notes; an Indian two-minute visit is different, which is why we will measure our own.

Why We Think It Is the Best Available Design, and What Could Prove Us Wrong

The case for it. Missing medicines are the most common history error[3], so the record must be written at every visit without effort. Invented clinical facts are the biggest risk of language models, so every line must be checked against the words spoken. Uncertainty must go to the doctor as a choice, because a guess that looks confident is worse than a question. No single component does all three; the combination does.

What is still unproven. Every result so far uses synthetic voices[1]. Real clinics are noisier, patients interrupt, and accents vary. Other Indian scribes are already in daily use[10], one of them now chosen by the National Health Authority for public facilities[11], and India still has no shared, real-world benchmark for comparing them[12]. Until 1Heath is measured on consented real consultations, "best" is a design argument, not a measured fact.

Beyond the Consultation

Emergency Card

Status: designed in the Patient app prototype; not built.

Blood group, allergies, long-term conditions, current medicines from every system and two family contacts, on the phone's lock screen and as a QR code, readable without unlocking. The patient chooses what it shows.

Family Health

Status: designed in the Patient app prototype; not built.

With each person's consent, a family's records link. When one member is diagnosed with an inherited condition such as thalassaemia trait, the doctor sees which relatives should be offered testing, with counselling first.

Watch Data

Status: planned.

Patients who connect a smartwatch can share sleep, steps, active minutes and resting heart rate as simple graphs, computed the same way for every brand, shown as context and never as a diagnosis.

What 1Heath Is Not

  • Not a diagnosis tool: the doctor diagnoses; 1Heath records and checks.
  • Not a replacement for clinical judgement: every prescription is the doctor's, signed by the doctor.
  • Not a data business: patient data is never sold.
  • Not ready for real patients yet: see what must happen first.
