In Short
  • The plan in one line: 1Heath becomes a licensed distributor for small and mid-size medicine makers. They supply 1Heath; 1Heath sells to clinic pharmacies that use the 1Heath apps, in one city first. The maker enters a new state without building a distributor network or a sales force.
  • How 1Heath earns: a trade margin on what it sells, set by medicine type and maker, never below 5% of 1Heath's selling price, and about 10 to 12% across everything (our target). The clinic still pays less than it pays its stockist today.
  • Why this, and not a fee: sellers do not pay software companies for orders; they give margin to whoever moves their medicines. India's largest listed distributor keeps about 10 paise of every rupee before costs and about 5 paise after its operating costs[1].
  • The three things that can sink it: money tied up in stock and credit, a weak maker selling a bad batch, and clinics moving only a small part of their buying to 1Heath. Each has a rule below.
  • The wall: the doctor's screen never shows price, margin or scheme. 1Heath sells to the pharmacy, never through the prescription[2].

How a Strip of Tablets Reaches a Clinic Today

Every medicine passes through four hands. The maker, stockist and chemist each keep a share of the price the patient finally pays (the MRP); the carrying and forwarding agent works for a fee.

  1. MakerManufactures and sets the price, within government price rules for controlled medicines.
  2. Carrying and forwarding agentUsually one per state for each company. Holds the company's stock and ships full cases to stockists. Works for a fee and does not own the goods.
  3. Stockist (distributor)Buys from many companies, delivers daily to chemists and clinics in its city, gives 1 to 4 weeks of credit and takes back expired stock. India has over 60,000 of them[3].
  4. Chemist or clinic pharmacySells or dispenses to the patient.

Stockist, price-controlled medicines8 paise of every rupee, set by lawCCI market study[4]
Chemist, price-controlled medicines16 paise, set by lawCCI market study[4]

A stockist's 9 or 10 paise is before its own costs of godown, staff, delivery, credit and expired stock. What it keeps after those is much smaller; we found no reliable public figure, so none is given here.

The First Idea, and What the Research Changed

The founding idea was simple: go straight to pharmaceutical companies, keep no warehouse, ship from the factory when a doctor orders, with free delivery above about ₹1,000 per company. Checking it against how the trade really works changed five things.

First ideaWhat we foundWhat we do instead
Ship from the factoryMakers ship full cases to their state agents, not six strips to a clinic.Order from licensed local stockists who already deliver daily.
Bypass the stockistsThe chemists' association has long opposed supply outside appointed stockists. The Competition Commission closed its 14-year case on this on 29 June 2026, finding no breach proved[7]. Its 2021 study found the old approval practice mostly gone, but still alive in pockets[4]. The legal risk is lower; the commercial risk of refusal remains.Work with the trade. Stockists gain orders; nobody is cut out.
A minimum of ₹1,000 per companyOne clinic's day of prescriptions touches 10 to 30 companies (our estimate). A minimum per company means many small parcels, most below the minimum.One delivery per clinic per run, one minimum per order, set in the pilot.
A large margin for 1HeathThe whole stockist share is about 9 to 10 paise per rupee[4], and it pays for delivery and credit.1Heath becomes the licensed distributor for small and mid-size makers and earns a trade margin of at least 5%, about 10 to 12% across everything. Who Already Does This, and What They Teach

CompanyWhat it doesThe lesson
PharmarackThe largest ordering platform between chemists and stockists: 12,000+ stockists and 2,50,000+ chemists, by its own count[3]."An app to order from stockists" already exists. 1Heath must not be just another one.
RetailioOrdering plus pay-later credit for chemists, through lending partners; claimed 1,00,000+ retailers and distributors in 2021[13].Credit is what chemists value, and lenders, not the app, should carry that risk.
SaveoBought medicines and sold them to pharmacies at a trading margin. ₹196 crore of revenue and a ₹38.5 crore loss in 2023–24[14]. Sold its distribution business in March 2026, then serving 10,000+ pharmacies[15].Owning stock is thin and cash-hungry. 1Heath starts small, only for clinics already in its apps, with credit from makers and a tight delivery area.

What none of them have. They see what a chemist orders. 1Heath sees what the doctor has just prescribed and what the clinic's shelf holds. Its job is to make sure the clinic never runs out, wastes little to expiry, and keeps the records the law asks for. Ordering follows from that.

The Model: 1Heath as the Distributor for Small Makers

Big companies sell only through their own agents and authorised stockists in each state, so a clinic cannot buy their brands directly[16]. Small and mid-size makers are different. Many make medicines in their own or contract plants and expand state by state through franchise distributors who do the selling locally[17]. Entering a new state costs them distributors, field staff and credit. 1Heath can be that route for them.

  1. 1Small or mid-size makerLicensed, quality-checked, supplies at an agreed price
  2. 21Heath, licensed distributorHolds stock in one licensed premises, sells and delivers
  3. 3Clinic pharmacyOrders in the Pharmacy app; a pharmacist dispenses
  4. 4PatientPays no more than the printed price, usually less

Online pharmacies show this is how the market is moving: Tata 1mg moved from a marketplace to buying directly from makers and licensed distributors, and now owns more than 90% of the medicines it sells[18]. One company already puts generic pharmacies inside clinics in three western states[19].

How Much 1Heath Keeps, by Type of Medicine

The margin is not one number. It depends on what the law allows for that medicine and on how much the maker saves by selling through 1Heath. All margins below are a share of 1Heath's selling price to the clinic. The floor is 5%.

Type of medicineWhat the rules and the trade allow today1Heath's margin (our policy)
Price-controlled medicines (national essential list)The ceiling price includes 16% for the chemist; the wholesaler's share is about 8%[20]. Nothing may be sold above the ceiling5%, the floor
Cancer medicines under the margin capTotal trade margin capped at 30% of the printed price for 137 medicines since 9 November 2026[5]5%, inside the 30% shared with the chemist
Branded generics from small and mid-size makersBy custom 10% for the wholesaler and 20% for the chemist[20]; in real price data the chemist's median is about 28%[20]8 to 12%. The maker can afford it because 1Heath replaces its distributor and field staff in the new state
Trade generics (sold on price, barely promoted)The trade often keeps most of the printed price: one example sells at ₹29 to the stockist against a printed price of ₹78[1][21]12 to 20%, while the clinic still pays clearly less than today
Medicines needing a cold chainHigher handling cost and spoilage riskAt least 7%
Devices, dressings and wellness productsDistribution margins are about 50 to 100% higher than for medicines[1]10 to 15%

The blended target is about 10 to 12% across everything sold (our assumption, to be measured in the pilot). It depends most on the mix: more trade generics raise it, more price-controlled medicines lower it.

Pricing Rules That Keep It Honest

  1. The clinic always pays less than today. Before a clinic switches, its last three stockist invoices are compared line by line; 1Heath lists a medicine only if it is cheaper.
  2. Never above the printed price or the ceiling price, and the clinic sells to patients at or below the printed price.
  3. One price list for every clinic of the same size, never a special deal for one doctor.
  4. Schemes such as "10 + 1 free" are shown only to the pharmacist, never on the doctor's screens.
  5. The margin never changes what the doctor sees. Brands on the prescribing screen are ordered by the doctor's own past choices, never by 1Heath's margin.

What the Margin Has to Pay For

Running costs as a share of 1Heath's medicine sales, at about ₹3.6 crore of sales a month

  1. Delivery to clinics in one cityour assumption1.5%
  2. Cost of giving clinics about 30 days to payabout 1% at 12% interest[1]1.0%
  3. Expired, damaged and returned stockour assumption1.0%
  4. Licensed premises, pharmacist and staffour assumption1.5%
  5. Payment fees and unpaid billsour assumption0.5%
  6. Total, before the 1Heath team and the AIabout 5.5%

So a blended margin of 11% leaves about 5.5% to pay for the team, the apps and the AI. Local distributors usually keep 2 to 4% of sales after their costs, and the largest listed one about 5%[1]. 1Heath can run leaner only because it delivers to a tight cluster of clinics that already order in its app.

The Hidden Cost: Money Tied Up

A distributor pays for stock before it sells and waits 25 to 40 days to be paid by pharmacies[1]. At the plan case's sales of about ₹43 crore a year, holding 30 days of stock and giving 30 days of credit would tie up about ₹7 crore. If makers are paid only after 45 days, or supply on consignment, that falls to about ₹1.8 crore. Credit from makers is a condition of signing them.

Which Makers 1Heath Will Sell For

Small makers carry the biggest quality risk. Under the stricter manufacturing rules (revised Schedule M), small makers had until 31 December 2025 to upgrade, and only about 1,700 of an estimated 6,500 had filed upgrade plans by late December[22]. In 2025, cough syrups from one small maker were linked to children's deaths and its licence was cancelled[23]. 1Heath's name will be on every delivery, so:

  • Only makers that meet revised Schedule M, with a valid manufacturing licence for each product.
  • A test certificate for every batch, and our own random tests at an accredited lab.
  • A written recall plan, and product liability insurance.
  • Any batch on the government's not-of-standard-quality list is blocked at once in every clinic.

How to Start Without a Big Warehouse

To hold and sell stock, 1Heath needs a wholesale drug licence for its premises and a qualified person in charge[12]. It does not need a big warehouse on day one.

A full city warehouse comes only when sales pass about ₹40 lakh a month. Foreign investors may not fund a company that sells its own stock to consumers online[24]; selling to licensed pharmacies is a different activity (our reading, to be confirmed by a lawyer).

How Ordering Stays Reliable

  • One catalogue. The national drug registry is the master list. Each stockist's item codes are mapped to it, so "Dolo 650" from three sellers is one product.
  • Stock read from the seller's own system. Most stockists run pharmacy accounting software. We read stock and price from it several times a day, and never promise what the seller does not show.
  • Orders split into lines. Each line moves through clear states: requested, confirmed, packed, dispatched, delivered, part-delivered, rejected, returned, credited. Every change is recorded with who and when.
  • No duplicates. A weak network never creates a second order by mistake.
  • The shelf count. Stock is counted in single tablets, because many clinics sell cut strips. It rises with each delivery received and falls with each signed prescription dispensed. Earliest-expiry stock is used first.
  • Daily reconciliation. Orders, deliveries, invoices and payments are matched every night; any mismatch goes to a person.
  • Tried before it is trusted. A trial run with a real partner stockist before any clinic depends on it, and a manual fallback for every step.

How the parts fit together is on the architecture and security and privacy pages.

The Wall Between Prescribing and Profit

  1. The doctor's screens never show price, margin, scheme or what 1Heath earns. Buying lives only in the Pharmacy app.
  2. Nothing the doctor sees is ranked by what anyone earns. No company can pay to appear first.
  3. No commission, gift or share ever goes to a doctor or clinic for prescribing; the ethics regulations forbid it[2].
  4. The margin is earned on what the pharmacy buys; what the doctor sees never changes with the brand, the margin or a maker's scheme.
  5. Generic names are shown on every prescription[2], and patients may buy anywhere.

Questions for the Lawyer, Before the First Order

  1. If 1Heath never owns the medicines, does it need any drug licence, and under which state's rules?
  2. As a licensed distributor, may 1Heath sell to a pharmacy owned by the prescribing doctor at a better price than other stockists, and what must be disclosed?
  3. When the clinic pharmacy is owned by the prescribing doctor, may stockists' discounts and "10 + 1 free" schemes reach it, given the ethics bar on rebates and commissions[2] and the 2024 pharmaceutical marketing code's bar on benefits to doctors[29]?
  4. How must payments flow so that 1Heath stays a software provider and not a payment aggregator[26]?
  5. If patients later order medicines from the Patient app through licensed online pharmacies, is a fee per order lawful while the e-pharmacy rules are still a draft[8]?
  6. Exactly what may a doctor without a retail licence stock and dispense in the pilot state today?
  7. What must a delivery partner meet for cold-chain items?
  8. For a later 1Heath generic range: contract manufacturing, brand registration and quality testing.
