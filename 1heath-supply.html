In Short
  • The plan in one line: 1Heath becomes the route to clinics for small and mid-size medicine makers, in one city first, in two stages. First a licensed partner stockist holds the stock and 1Heath earns a fixed fee per order. Then, once the numbers are proven, 1Heath becomes the licensed distributor in its own name.
  • How 1Heath earns as distributor: a trade margin set by medicine type and maker. It is 5% at the lowest, and only on price-controlled medicines paid for on delivery. Everything else carries at least 8%, about 13% across everything in both of our cases (our target), because the clinic's own choice of brands leans to small makers' brands with wider margins.
  • Why clinics move their buying: a small clinic earns from its own pharmacy counter. Buying cheaper from 1Heath leaves it more of the printed price on every strip, which is what makes the doctor's membership worth paying (worked example).
  • What it costs: about 10% of sales for a small new distributor (our estimate; outside reviewers said 9.5 to 13%). India's largest listed distributor keeps about 10 paise of every rupee and about 4 paise after its running costs[1][2]. So owning stock leaves 1Heath about 3 paise a rupee at a 13% margin, and nothing at 10%.
  • Where the 13% comes from: by custom the carrying and forwarding agent takes about 3% and the wholesaler 10%[3]; even the government's own generic chain pays its distributors 10% and its shops 20%[4]. Buying straight from the maker lets 1Heath do both jobs and keep both cuts. A plain wholesaler buying from another distributor keeps only 1 to 2% after costs[5].
  • The real price to beat: a month of three common diabetes, blood-pressure and cholesterol medicines costs about ₹495 as leading brands and about ₹105 at a Jan Aushadhi store (table). A saving must reach the patient, not only the clinic.
  • The three things that can sink it: money tied up in stock (plan on no credit from makers), a bad batch from a weak maker, and clinics moving only a small part of their buying. Each has a rule below.
  • The wall: the doctor's screen never shows price, margin or scheme. 1Heath buys and resells in its own name, never as a maker's agent, and sells to the pharmacy, never through the prescription[6][7].

How a Strip of Tablets Reaches a Clinic Today

Every medicine passes through four hands. The maker, stockist and chemist each keep a share of the price the patient finally pays (the MRP); the carrying and forwarding agent works for a fee.

  1. MakerManufactures and sets the price, within government price rules for controlled medicines.
  2. Carrying and forwarding agentUsually one per state for each company. Holds the company's stock and ships full cases to stockists. Works for a fee and does not own the goods.
  3. Stockist (distributor)Buys from many companies, delivers daily to chemists and clinics in its city, gives 1 to 4 weeks of credit and takes back expired stock. India has over 60,000 of them[8].
  4. Chemist or clinic pharmacySells or dispenses to the patient.

Stockist, price-controlled medicines8 paise of every rupee, set by lawCCI market study[9]
Chemist, price-controlled medicines16 paise, set by lawCCI market study[9]
Stockist and chemist, other medicinesAbout 10 and 20 paise by custom; measured at about 9 and 28 paiseCCI market study[9]

"Trade generics" (lightly promoted brands sold mostly through the trade)Much larger trade margins; their MRP is often no lower than branded genericsCCI market study[9]

A stockist's 9 or 10 paise is before its own costs of godown, staff, delivery, credit and expired stock. What it keeps after those is much smaller; we found no reliable public figure, so none is given here.

The First Idea, and What the Research Changed

The founding idea was simple: go straight to pharmaceutical companies, keep no warehouse, ship from the factory when a doctor orders, with free delivery above about ₹1,000 per company. Checking it against how the trade really works changed five things.

First ideaWhat we foundWhat we do instead
Ship from the factoryMakers ship full cases to their state agents, not six strips to a clinic.Order from licensed local stockists who already deliver daily.
Bypass the stockistsThe chemists' association has long opposed supply outside appointed stockists. The Competition Commission closed its 14-year case on this on 29 June 2026, finding no breach proved[12]. Its 2021 study found the old approval practice mostly gone, but still alive in pockets[9]. The legal risk is lower; the commercial risk of refusal remains.Work with the trade. Stockists gain orders; nobody is cut out.

What none of them have. They see what a chemist orders. 1Heath sees what the doctor has just prescribed and what the clinic's shelf holds. Its job is to make sure the clinic never runs out, wastes little to expiry, and keeps the records the law asks for. Ordering follows from that.

The Model: 1Heath as the Route for Small Makers

Big companies sell only through their own agents and authorised stockists in each state, so a clinic cannot buy their brands directly[26]. Small and mid-size makers are different. Many make medicines in their own or contract plants and expand state by state through franchise distributors who do the selling locally[27]. Entering a new state costs them distributors, field staff and credit. 1Heath can be that route for them, in two stages.

  1. 1Small or mid-size makerLicensed, quality-checked, sells at an agreed price
  2. 2Stage 1: partner stockist. Stage 2: 1HeathThe licensed wholesaler that owns the stock, stores it, invoices and delivers
  3. 3Clinic pharmacyOrders in the Pharmacy app; a pharmacist dispenses
  4. 4PatientPays no more than the printed price

The clinic can order its whole basket, big brands included.
2. 1Heath as licensed distributor1Heath, bought from the maker and resold in its own nameThe trade margin in the table belowOnly when a city shows a blended margin of about 13%, enough monthly sales to cover a licensed premises, written terms from makers and a lawyer's opinion. See how to start.

Buy and resell, never act as the maker's agent. Under the Drugs Act, a seller of a bad batch has a defence if it bought from a licensed source, could not have known and stored it properly. That defence is not open to "the manufacturer … or his agent for the distribution thereof"[6]. So 1Heath buys stock outright and sells it as its own, even when it also helps the maker find clinics.

Online pharmacies show the market moving this way: Tata 1mg moved from a marketplace to buying directly from makers and licensed distributors, and now owns more than 90% of the medicines it sells[28]. One company already puts generic pharmacies inside clinics in three western states[29].

Pricing Rules That Keep It Honest

  1. The clinic pays less than today, compared like for like. Before a clinic switches, its last three invoices are compared on the same medicine, strength, pack, expiry, credit and tax. Cheaper is the aim, not a promise to undercut every stockist.
  2. Never above the printed price or the ceiling price, and the clinic sells to patients at or below the printed price.
  3. One published price list for every licensed buyer of the same size, never a special deal for one doctor.
  4. Every discount and scheme goes on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business, never to a person, and never on the doctor's screens[33].
  5. The margin never changes what the doctor sees. Brands on the prescribing screen are ordered by the doctor's own past choices, never by 1Heath's margin or stock.

What the Margin Has to Pay For

India's largest listed distributor, Entero, sold ₹6,591 crore in 2025–26, kept about 10% as gross margin and earned 4.03% before interest, tax and depreciation[2][1]. Its running costs were about 6% of sales, with 102 warehouses behind it[34]. A small new distributor in one city will spend more.

Running costs of a small new distributor, as a share of its medicine sales (our estimate)

  1. Delivery, failed drops and returns tripssmall evening-clinic orders2.5%
  2. Licensed premises, pharmacist and store staff2.0%
  3. Staff who sign up clinics and manage makerssmall makers have no sales force in the new state1.5%
  4. Interest on money tied up in stock and creditabout 60 days of sales2.0%
  5. Expired, damaged and lost stock1.0%
  6. Quality tests, insurance and compliance0.5%
  7. Payment fees, unpaid bills and stock software0.5%
  8. Total, before the 1Heath team and the AIabout 10%

Our first figure of 5.5% left out sales staff, quality tests and interest; outside reviewers put a small new distributor at 9.5 to 13%. Both our cases use 10%. At a 13% margin, owning stock leaves about 3 paise a rupee for the team and the AI; at 16%, about 6 paise. Traders on Quora report the same squeeze: a plain wholesaler keeps about 10% gross and 1 to 2% net[5], so the 13% only holds if 1Heath buys direct from makers and skips layers (their accounts).

The Hidden Cost: Money Tied Up

A distributor pays for stock before it sells and waits 25 to 40 days to be paid by pharmacies[31]. Small makers rarely give a new distributor credit; we now plan on none, and count any credit from makers as a bonus once it is signed and has run for a few months. In the founder's case at 1,000 doctors, 1Heath sells about ₹44 crore a year from its own stock; with about 60 days of sales in stock and credit, as large distributors carry, that ties up about ₹7.3 crore. At 20,000 doctors the same rule gives about ₹145 crore, which is why stage 2 waits for profits or a partner's balance sheet. Credit to clinics stays short at first, with a limit for each.

Which Makers 1Heath Will Sell For

Small makers carry the biggest quality risk. Under the stricter manufacturing rules for allopathic medicines (revised Schedule M), small makers had until 31 December 2025 to upgrade, and only about 1,700 of an estimated 6,500 had filed upgrade plans by late December[35]. In 2025, cough syrups from one small maker were linked to children's deaths and its licence was cancelled[36]. In 2024–25, 3,104 of 1.16 lakh drug samples tested were not of standard quality and 245 were spurious or adulterated[37]. 1Heath's name will be on every delivery, so:

  • Allopathic: only makers that meet revised Schedule M, checked at the plant, with a valid licence for each product, and a lab test for harmful solvents on every batch of syrup.
  • Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani: only makers that meet revised Schedule T[32], and tests for heavy metals and hidden allopathic drugs on new makers and on herbo-mineral medicines.
  • Homeopathy: sealed packs from licensed homeopathic makers only.
  • A test certificate for every batch, checked against the lab that issued it, and our own random tests at an accredited lab, more often for new and riskier makers.
  • A written recall plan, practised once before the first sale; product liability insurance; part of each maker's payment held back for returns and recalls.
  • Any batch on the government's not-of-standard-quality list is blocked at once in every clinic, and a person checks the match.

Doctors' trust is the real gate. An eye surgeon on Quora writes that he has "lost all faith in the generic drug market"[38]. So the doctor, not a pharmacist, chooses the maker. Each product shows its maker, plant and latest batch test, and price alone is never the pitch.

A patient on metformin, telmisartan and atorvastatin pays about ₹495 a month for the brands, about ₹396 online, and about ₹105 at a Jan Aushadhi store. That gap is far larger than the 10% a local chemist or the 20% an online seller takes off. There are more than 18,000 Jan Aushadhi stores[46], up from 14,589 at the end of 2024[4], so they, more than online sellers, are the real low-price benchmark.

Needs Pawan: who gets the saving? If 1Heath sells to a clinic for less but the printed price stays the same, the clinic earns more and the patient saves nothing. Small makers' brands can also carry high printed prices with wide margins, which is where a clinic counter earns most. Our recommendation: every product on 1Heath's list has a printed price no higher than a fixed limit set against Jan Aushadhi and leading-brand prices; a low-price option sits beside each brand; and refills are priced below the online price. The clinic still earns its normal shop margin, and the patient sees a real saving.

How to Start Without a Warehouse

Stage 1 needs no premises: the partner stockist holds the licence and the stock. To hold and sell stock itself in stage 2, 1Heath needs a wholesale drug licence for its premises and a qualified person in charge[17], plus homeopathic and AYUSH sale licences for those lines[18][20].

When 1Heath's own licensed premises pays for itself (our estimate)

  1. Rent, pharmacist, two delivery staff and one store hand₹2.25 lakh a month
  2. Margin left after delivery, credit, expiry and payments9% or 13% margin, less about 5%4% or 8%
  3. Monthly sales needed to cover the premises₹56 lakh or ₹28 lakh

With 30 clinics each buying ₹60,000 a month through 1Heath, sales would be ₹18 lakh, not enough. So the premises waits for real order volume through the partner.

The test for moving to stage 2, all five together: a blended margin of about 13% on products clinics actually reorder; ₹28 to ₹56 lakh of monthly orders in one cluster; makers' terms in writing; a lawyer's opinion on the membership and supply together (see below); and the licences in hand. Foreign investors may not fund a company that sells its own stock to consumers online[47]; selling to licensed pharmacies is a different activity (our reading, to be confirmed by a lawyer).

How Ordering Stays Reliable

  • One catalogue. The national drug registry is the master list. Each stockist's item codes are mapped to it, so "Dolo 650" from three sellers is one product.
  • Stock read from the seller's own system. Most stockists run pharmacy accounting software. We read stock and price from it several times a day, and never promise what the seller does not show.
  • Orders split into lines. Each line moves through clear states: requested, confirmed, packed, dispatched, delivered, part-delivered, rejected, returned, credited. Every change is recorded with who and when.
  • No duplicates. A weak network never creates a second order by mistake.
  • The shelf count. Stock is counted in single tablets, because many clinics sell cut strips. It rises with each delivery received and falls with each signed prescription dispensed. Earliest-expiry stock is used first.
  • Daily reconciliation. Orders, deliveries, invoices and payments are matched every night; any mismatch goes to a person.
  • Tried before it is trusted. A trial run with a real partner stockist before any clinic depends on it, and a manual fallback for every step.

How the parts fit together is on the architecture and security and privacy pages.

The Wall Between Prescribing and Profit

  1. The doctor's screens never show price, margin, scheme, stock or what 1Heath earns. Buying lives only in the Pharmacy app.
  2. Nothing the doctor sees is ranked by what anyone earns, or by what 1Heath stocks. No company can pay to appear first.
  3. No commission, gift or share ever goes to a doctor or clinic for prescribing; the ethics regulations forbid it[7]. The marketing code also binds distributors: no benefit in kind to anyone who prescribes or supplies[33].
  4. The Doctor app costs the same for every doctor, whether or not the clinic buys from 1Heath, and no feature depends on buying.
  5. The supply business sees orders, quantities and batches, never patients' names or prescriptions.
  6. Generic names are shown on every prescription[7], and patients may buy anywhere.

What the Pilot Will Measure

Three numbers decide this model, and only real invoices and paid orders can give them: the margin on medicines clinics actually reorder, the share of a clinic's buying that really moves, and the credit makers actually give. Interviews come first, with stockists, then 20 to 30 clinics' purchase records, then makers.

Questions for the Lawyer, Before the First Order

  1. If 1Heath never owns the medicines, does it need any drug licence, and under which state's rules?
