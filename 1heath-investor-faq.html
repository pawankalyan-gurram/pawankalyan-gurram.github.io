For the Technical and AI Team

What exactly is built today?

A working Android Doctor app, as a demo. The doctor records the consultation; speech goes through our gateway to Sarvam's speech service; a language model drafts the note and prescription; a rules engine on the phone checks every line; the doctor signs with a slide and gets a prescription PDF. The app is Flutter and Kotlin, the rules engine is Python running on the phone, and the gateway runs on Cloudflare. The front desk, pharmacy and patient apps and the shared record server are designed, not built. Status: built, as a demo (architecture).

How do you stop the AI from inventing things?

Rules decide; the model only phrases. Facts heard in the conversation go into a ledger, the draft is checked line by line against what was said and against the record, a medicine name the system cannot match blocks signing, and a prescription line can come only from the doctor's own words. The app never suggests a medicine. Status: built and tested on simulated consultations (models and data).

How accurate is it, and how far can we trust the numbers?

On 14 unseen simulated consultations in Hindi and Telugu: about 65% of clinical facts captured, 54 of 85 medicine lines matched strictly with the right name offered for 6 more, and no invented allergy, refused medicine or wrong dose reaching a note unflagged. That is a small synthetic test: zero errors in 14 cases is still consistent with a true error rate of about 1 in 5. Nothing has been tested on real patients yet. Status: measured, small.

What if Sarvam raises prices, changes its models or goes away?

Speech recognition sits behind one interface in the app, so another service or our own model can replace it without rewriting the flow. Alternatives are priced and listed, from cheaper cloud services to open models with free licences that we can host in India, and the drafting model's weights are open. We plan our own medical speech model within one to two years. Status: designed for, not yet switched (cost routes, own model).

What does the AI cost per visit?

About ₹3.09 on our 3.5 to 5 minute test consultations, almost all of it speech recognition. A typical 2-minute visit would cost about ₹1 to ₹1.75 today. Routes below ₹1 (one pass, a volume plan, a cheaper service, an open model on our own servers, recognition on the phone) are estimated, not tested. At ₹3.09 for five years the founder's case loses about ₹75 crore instead of ₹20 crore, so this is a condition, not an extra. Status: measured, then target.

Does it work with poor internet?

The demo needs internet to transcribe. If drafting fails or takes more than 10 seconds, the doctor gets a rules-only note in which every medicine must be confirmed. An offline mode is not built; whether small clinics need one is a pilot question, and recognition on the phone would give it. Status: partly handled.

Will it scale?

On the speech service's pay-as-you-go plan, about six doctors can speak at the same moment before a higher plan is needed; higher plans allow far more, and our own servers remove the limit. The shared record is designed to run on a server in India. Load has not been tested beyond the demo. Status: estimated.

Who owns the code and the intellectual property?

The founder wrote the code; it will be assigned to the company at incorporation, with the name and trademarks. The app uses only free and open licences, and the drafting model's weights are under Apache 2.0. No patents are filed. Status: to be done at incorporation.

What is the data advantage over time?

Two things no general model has: consented, doctor-checked recordings of real Indian clinic consultations in many languages, which train our own medical speech model; and records that follow patients across visits. Both start only with consent and ethics approval, from the pilot on. Status: not yet started.

How is patient data protected?

In the demo, clinical text stays on the phone. Before any real patient: consent under the DPDP Act, a server and gateway in India, written no-retention and no-training terms from the speech provider, access by role (the pharmacy sees the prescription, never the note), a CERT-In incident plan, and audio deleted after signing unless the patient consented to keep it for training. Security audits and ISO 27001 come before scale. Status: designed, not built (security and privacy).

For the Clinical and Product Team

Is it safe for patients?

Not yet shown. Before release: ethics committee approval, registration on the Clinical Trials Registry of India, at least 50 consented real consultations each checked by an independent doctor, and a stop rule that pauses testing on any unflagged allergy, dose or medicine-name error. Status: not yet shown.

What does the doctor still decide?

Everything clinical. The doctor speaks, reviews and signs; the app records and checks what was said, flags clashes with the record, and never suggests a diagnosis or a medicine. It cannot judge whether the doctor's decision is right.

What do the safety checks catch?

A medicine the patient is allergic to, a dose outside the usual range, a child's dose without a weight, a repeated medicine, a line the doctor did not say, and a medicine name it cannot match. Only the founder can add or weaken a safety check, and checks are never paid extras.

Which languages work?

The speech service supports English and 22 Indian languages. 1Heath itself is tested only on simulated Hindi and Telugu so far. Each further language needs its own test before we claim it.

How do you handle Ayurveda and homeopathy?

About half of Indians used an AYUSH system in the past year, so the record holds medicines from every system as the patient reports them. We do not claim checks for interactions across systems until validated data exist (systems of medicine).

Does it save the doctor time?

Not measured. The pilot measures time per visit, time spent correcting the draft, and whether doctors still use it after four weeks.

Who is accountable clinically?

The signing doctor for each prescription. For the product, a clinical safety lead and a pharmacist are the first hires, named before any real patient. No clinical adviser is named yet; that is a gap we state openly.

For the Business Model and Commercial Team

Who pays, and for what?

Doctors whose clinic has its own pharmacy pay a membership of ₹199 to ₹499 a month (₹299 in our cases; the lowest our costs allow, set after the pilot). They pay for cheaper medicines for the clinic pharmacy; the scribe, record and checks come included. Any licensed pharmacy can buy through 1Heath, but members pay less; 1Heath earns a fixed fee per order from a partner stockist first, then a trade margin as the licensed distributor. Labs pay a flat monthly fee. Insurers may later pay a fee per claim the patient asks to send (who pays).

Will doctors really pay?

Doctors told us they would not pay for software alone, because it mostly helps the patient, but would pay a monthly membership that gets their clinic pharmacy cheaper medicines, with the software included. Nobody has paid yet. The pilot target is that at least 40% of active doctors pay ₹299 after a free month. Status: spoken, not paid.

Why would a clinic buy medicines through 1Heath?

Many small clinics run their own pharmacy counter, and a lower buying price adds straight to what the counter keeps. One rural study found 82% of practices dispense; no city survey exists, so the pilot counts it. Status: published, narrow (medicine supply).

Does the patient save anything?

Yes, by our own policy: 1Heath lists only products whose maker's printed price is at most three times the Jan Aushadhi price for the same medicine, strength and pack, with a low-price option beside each brand. We do not change any printed price. A month of three common diabetes, blood-pressure and cholesterol medicines costs about ₹495 as leading brands and about ₹105 at Jan Aushadhi (prices).

Is there a conflict between helping write prescriptions and selling medicines?

Yes, and we treat it as the biggest ethical risk. The wall: the Doctor app never shows prices, brands or margins; the membership is a flat monthly fee, never linked to how much the clinic buys or what the doctor prescribes; member and non-member prices are published and the same for every buyer of the same size; no doctor is ever paid for sales; supply runs in a separate company once it starts; and a lawyer reviews the whole model against NMC rules and the 2024 marketing code before the first order (the wall).

How do you reach doctors, and at what cost?

Doctor to doctor, through medical colleges, medical associations and specialty societies, with a 15-minute demo on the doctor's own phone. First customer: Telugu-speaking general physicians and paediatricians in small private clinics in one city. Target: under ₹20,000 per active doctor. Status: target (go to market).

What does one doctor bring in?

In the founder's case, about ₹66,660 a year per active doctor kept by 1Heath once it holds its own stock, or about ₹16,050 through partner stockists. Both rest on assumptions about clinic buying that the pilot must replace with invoices (both cases).

For the Finance Team

How much does it lose, and when is it profitable?

Both cases lose ₹6 to ₹7 crore a year at 1,000 doctors. The founder's case (20,000 doctors by year 5) loses about ₹20 crore over five years, at worst about ₹25 crore, and makes about ₹4.9 crore in year 5. The cautious case (10,000 doctors) loses about ₹50 crore and is still losing in year 5. Status: model (five years).

Which assumptions move the result most?

The AI cost per visit and the supply margin. At the measured ₹3.09 a visit the founder's case loses about ₹75 crore over five years. Each 3 points of supply margin moves the result at 1,000 doctors by about ₹1.3 crore. The membership price barely moves it (levers).

How much money is tied up in stock?

None while partner stockists hold the stock. Once 1Heath holds its own, about ₹7.3 crore at 1,000 doctors and about ₹145 crore at 20,000, at 60 days. That stage starts only after partner-stockist invoices prove the numbers.

How is revenue counted?

When 1Heath owns the stock, the full medicine sale is revenue under Ind AS 115, so revenue looks large (about ₹675 crore in year 5) while what 1Heath keeps is far smaller (about ₹100 crore). We report both, and judge the business on what is kept.

What does the first year cost?

About ₹3.29 crore if all 11 people work the whole year. The team starts with five and adds people, such as a security engineer, as the work needs them, so the real first-year cost should be lower. The sheet: engineering and AI ₹1.20 crore, the clinical team ₹0.70 crore, management ₹0.40 crore, and the rest on security, legal, launch and running costs (cost sheet). The amount we raise and the terms are shared privately.

What about tax and grants?

The start-up tax holiday continues under section 140 of the Income-tax Act 2025. Clinics are exempt from GST, so a consultation-only clinic cannot claim back GST on our fee. The largest seed grant scheme is closed; smaller incubator grants remain open (finance).

Can we check the model ourselves?

Yes. The full model is a short Python file you can run: the business model file.

For the Legal and Regulatory Team

Is it a medical device?

Probably, for the safety checks. Our reading of CDSCO's 2026 guidance is Class A or B, but it is not settled and could be higher. We will ask CDSCO in writing before any real patient. Class A registration is online and quick; a Class B licence can take up to 140 days under the current rules, plus queries, so it is applied for in month 1 and could delay the release (device).

Are you compliant with the DPDP Act?

Not yet; nothing real is collected. The consent flow, notices in the patient's language, withdrawal, guardian consent and breach plans are designed to meet the Act and its 2025 Rules, whose main duties start on 13 May 2027, and the IT Act's sensitive-data rules that apply until then (data).

Can a clinic sell medicines?

A doctor may hand their own patients medicines under Schedule K, but only within its conditions: no shop open to the public, no sale across the counter, medicines bought only from licensed sellers, and records of what was bought with batch numbers, open to inspection. A clinic pharmacy that sells like a shop needs a retail drug licence and a pharmacist. We check and keep each buyer's licence and arrangement before the first order, and the app never treats Schedule K as a shop licence.

Which licences does supply need?

In stage 1, the partner stockist holds the wholesale licence and the stock. In stage 2, 1Heath's separate supply company holds its own, only after stage 1 proves the numbers. Small makers from other states are signed up from the first phase; who holds their stock at first is still to be decided. Patient delivery needs a separate opinion (who may sell).

Is the company incorporated?

Not yet. Incorporation, with the code and the name assigned to the company, comes before any investment.

Where is the full list of legal steps?

On one page, each with its status and what it blocks: legal readiness. Today none is complete, which is why there are no real patients and no medicine orders yet.

For the Market and Competition Team

How big is the market?

About 12 lakh working allopathic doctors in India (8 in 10 of the 15.35 lakh registered in July 2026); about 66,000 in small private clinics in the two Telugu states, where we start; and a target of 4,000 doctors in three years. The numbers and their sources are on the go-to-market page.

Why won't Eka Care or HealthPlix win?

They may. Eka Care's scribe is in daily use and was chosen by the National Health Authority for public-sector doctors; HealthPlix's tool already drafts prescriptions and checks drug interactions. On features, there is little between us. 1Heath must win on accuracy measured in real clinics, every system of medicine on one record, and the signed prescription reaching the pharmacy and patient. Until the pilot measures that, it is a claim (competitors).

What about Medkart, Jan Aushadhi and online pharmacies?

Medkart runs pharmacies inside clinics and shares profit with doctors; we do not pay doctors anything. More than 20,000 Jan Aushadhi stores[4] set the low price, so our price cap is set against them. Online pharmacies take 15 to 20% off brands; refills through 1Heath must beat that or be faster.

What about O Health?

A direct competitor. Reported in November 2026 with backing from Apollo Hospitals' joint managing director, it turns the doctor-patient conversation into notes with its own medical speech recognition running in the clinic[5]. Where we differ: a checked prescription the doctor signs, Indian languages for small clinics first, one record shared with the pharmacy and the patient, and every system of medicine on it. Whether that is enough is for the pilot to show.

What is the moat?

Not proven yet. The candidates: accuracy on real Indian clinic speech, our own medical speech model trained on consented recordings, records that follow patients, and a working supply route for small clinics. Each depends on the pilot.

For the Team and Governance Review

Who is building it?

Pawan Kalyan, the founder, designed and built the working Doctor app (experience) while working at Zoho. He will resign from Zoho and work full-time on 1Heath once the first investment is committed. First hires: a clinical safety lead, a pharmacist and engineers for speech and the record. Clinical, pharmacy and legal advisers are planned by speciality; none is named yet.

Can a small team really build everything in three months?

Counted from the day the team starts (nothing has started yet), and for a fixed scope, yes: the Doctor app already works, so the build starts from a working core. The real risks are approvals and accuracy on real voices, so every approval starts in week 1, and the release date moves if accuracy is below the bar (six months from the team's start).

What kind of investor are you looking for?

One from healthcare, pharma or hospitals, who brings ground knowledge and a network: hospitals, medicine makers, lawyers, and central and state health programmes. 1Heath has to work with hospitals, small makers, state drug controllers and ABDM, and the right investor shortens each of those roads. We look first for investors who know healthcare, because that knowledge is worth more to us than the money. The amount and terms are shared privately.

Could a drug company or a hospital group own shares?

Later, yes: a large medicine maker or hospital group may hold a minority stake, because their knowledge and reach help. Because 1Heath drafts prescriptions and supplies clinic pharmacies, these rules come with it: no say over the medicine list, the order of brands or the safety rules; no access to prescription or patient data; no exclusive deal that shuts out other makers or hospitals; an independent safety and neutrality committee; brand neutrality checked and published; and the shareholding stated openly on this site. A lawyer reviews it against the 2024 pharma marketing code and NMC rules before anything is signed.

What will you never do?

Sell patient data; sell doctors' prescribing data to drug companies; pay doctors for sales or referrals; let prices or brands appear on the Doctor app; or claim results we have not measured.

What could kill the company?

A safety failure on real patients; doctors who use it free but will not pay; clinics that will not move their buying; a supply margin below about 13%; AI cost that stays near ₹3 a visit; or a legal opinion that the membership and supply together are an inducement. Each is a milestone in the first year (what is proven and what is not).

Questions We Cannot yet Answer with Evidence

These are the questions a review of this page said investors will press hardest. Each has a plan, not yet proof; we will replace each answer with evidence as it comes.

Who are the named clinical owners?

Not named yet. Before any real patient: a clinical safety lead who can stop a release, a pharmacist, a principal investigator for the ethics protocol, and independent doctors who check notes. Each with written duties and time committed.

What is the clinical validation protocol?

Being written for the ethics committee. Planned endpoints: misses and errors for allergies, medicine name and strength, dose, frequency, duration, negatives and children's weight, reported separately by language; time per visit and correction time. At least 50 consultations in a feasibility stage, then a larger study before claiming safety. Fifty is a first gate, not proof.

What is the error rate by type, not just overall?

Only overall figures exist, from 14 simulated consultations. Error rates by type and language need real consultations and come from the pilot.

Who is liable if something dangerous is missed?

The signing doctor for each prescription; 1Heath for the software. Before release we need professional and product liability insurance, an incident process (who investigates, who tells the patient, who fixes it) and clinic terms that set out each side's duties. None of this is in place yet.

How do you know the record belongs to the right patient?

Designed, not built: the desk confirms identity at each visit; ABHA where the patient has it; shared family phones and changed numbers handled by a person-level record, not a phone-level one; duplicates merged only by a person; outside prescriptions entered as reported. The pilot tests this in real clinics.

What makes the prescription legally authentic?

Not settled. The demo's slide to sign is not enough. Before real prescribing: the doctor verified against the national registry, a legally reviewed electronic signature, the signed version locked, and every change and correction kept in an audit trail.

What happens if a patient withdraws consent to audio training?

The recording and its labels are deleted. What a model already trained has learned cannot be removed, and the consent form says so before the patient agrees (own model).

Where is the proof that supply works?

There is none yet: no stockist or maker has signed, and no clinic has ordered. The pilot needs one partner stockist with written terms (service levels, returns of expired stock, recalls, batch records, credit) and real invoices.

What are the real adoption numbers?

None yet. The pilot measures how many invited doctors start, use it daily, still use it after four weeks and pay after the free month, and the cost of winning and supporting each doctor.

Has any insurer agreed to the claims workflow?

No. The national claims exchange carries claim information; it does not by itself pay software companies. A fee needs a signed insurer or claims-processor contract and a lawyer's confirmation that 1Heath is a technology supplier, not an unregistered third-party administrator. It is not in our numbers.
