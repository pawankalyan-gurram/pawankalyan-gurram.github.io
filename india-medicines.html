Medicines in India: Quality, Antibiotics, Prices and the Pharmacy

How good, rational and affordable the medicines Indians take are, how antibiotics are misused, and what a signed prescription changes.

In Short
  • India makes about 20% of the world's generic medicines and about 60% of UNICEF's vaccines[1].
  • At home, 76% of pharmacies in one city sold antibiotics without a prescription[2], and most antibiotics used are ones WHO says to use sparingly[3].
  • Drug-resistant bacteria directly caused about 2.14 lakh deaths in India in 2019[4].
  • Medicines alone pushed 3.8 crore Indians into poverty in a year[5]; more than 20,000 government generic stores sell at what the government says are 50–80% below brand prices[6].

The Pharmacy of the World

3rdIndia's rank in medicine production by volume (11th by value)Government factsheet, 2026[1]
~20%Of the world's generic medicines, from over 3,000 companies and 10,500 unitsGovernment factsheet, 2026[1]
~60%Of UNICEF's vaccines; 90% of WHO's measles vaccine needsGovernment factsheet, 2026[1]

Exports were about ₹2.6 lakh crore in 2024–25[1] and a little higher in 2025–26, still about ₹2.6 lakh crore[7] (both converted from US dollars at ₹84.6 per dollar). The question for patients in India is different: are the medicines they get good, rational, affordable and correctly dispensed?

Are Medicines in India Good Quality?

India's largest drug quality survey (2014–16) tested 47,012 samples of 224 medicines in 654 districts[8].

The survey is ten years old. Routine testing since then gives a similar picture: in 2024–25, regulators tested 1.16 lakh samples and found 3,104 not of standard quality (about 2.7%) and 245 spurious or adulterated (about 0.2%)[9]. These samples are not random: inspectors pick suspect products, so the shares are not a national rate.

How Antibiotics Are Used

54.9%Of antibiotic doses in 2019 were WHO "Watch" drugs, meant to be used sparingly; WHO's target is at least 60% "Access" drugsLancet Regional Health SE Asia, 2022[3]
48.7%Of antibiotic fixed-dose combinations were combinations WHO discouragesSame study[3]
76%Of 200 pharmacies in Coimbatore sold antibiotics to a simulated patient without a prescription (82% for a cold, 70% for diarrhoea)IJCMPH, 2025[2]
2.14 lakhDeaths in India in 2019 directly caused by drug-resistant bacteria; 6.87 lakh more associated with themGRAM, The Lancet, 2024[4]

The law already expects a prescription. Since 2014, 46 drugs in Schedule H1, including many antibiotics and anti-TB drugs, may be sold only with a prescription recorded in a separate register (prescriber, patient, drug, quantity) kept for three years, and must carry a red Rx label[10]. The register assumes a prescription exists. In practice it often does not.

Can People Afford Their Medicines?

3.8 croreIndians pushed into poverty in one year by spending on medicines alone (5.5 crore by all health spending)PHFI study in The BMJ, 2018[5]
20,149Jan Aushadhi generic medicine stores (30 June 2026), selling 2,110 medicines and 315 surgical items at prices the government puts at 50–80% below brands; the target is 25,000 by March 2027Lok Sabha reply, July 2026[6]

India has nearly 60,000 generic brands[1], so the same molecule is often sold under many names at different prices. Printing the generic name beside the brand on every prescription lets the patient and pharmacist choose a cheaper equivalent; India's medical ethics regulations already ask doctors to prescribe by generic name "as far as possible"[11].

The Pharmacist's Place

India has about 16 lakh registered pharmacists and adds about 3 lakh graduates a year[12]. Pharmacists dispense against a slip of paper: they rarely see the diagnosis, the patient's allergies or what else the patient takes.

What This Means for 1Heath

  • 1

    Create the prescription the law assumes. A signed digital prescription (built in the demo) gives the Schedule H1 register something real to record.

  • 2

    Print the generic name. Every line shows the generic in capitals beside the brand, so the cheaper equivalent is visible.

  • 3

    Flag irrational combinations. The demo checks each prescription against a list of 711 banned combinations taken from a public dataset of uncertain provenance and used only for the demo (see models and data).

  • 4

    Give the pharmacist what they need, and no more. In the planned Pharmacy app, the pharmacy sees the signed prescription, never the consultation note.
