Who Pays 1Heath, and What Similar Companies Earn

In Short
  • This page is who pays: each payer, the stages and Indian benchmarks. The costs, both cases and the five-year path are on Business Model: The Numbers.
  • The model: doctors whose clinic has its own pharmacy pay a membership of ₹199 to ₹499 a month (₹299 in our cases; the lowest our costs allow, set after the pilot). They pay for cheaper buying: the clinic pharmacy gets medicines, surgical items, injections and IV fluids through 1Heath for less and keeps a bigger margin. The AI scribe, the record and the safety checks come included. Any licensed pharmacy can buy too, but members pay less. 1Heath earns on that supply: first a fixed fee per order from a partner stockist, then its own trade margin as the licensed distributor. Labs pay a flat monthly fee.
  • Why it works on the ground: a small clinic earns from its own pharmacy as much as from consultations, so a doctor who will not pay for software alone, because it mostly helps the patient, will pay ₹199 to ₹499 for a membership that saves the clinic pharmacy several times that.
  • Why supply carries it: every company that earns from medicine supply in India earns as a distributor or retailer[1][2], while low-priced clinic software has not paid for itself[3][4].
  • How thin it is: India's largest listed distributor keeps about 10 paise of each rupee and earns about 4 paise after running costs[2][5]. 1Heath's cases keep about 13 paise on small makers' brands and spend about 10, so the margin must rise towards 16 paise.

Why medicines carry the model: Indians bought medicines worth about ₹2.41 lakh crore in 2025[8], and more than half of that is sold through standalone pharmacies[9], including counters inside small clinics. Each prescription signed in 1Heath is a purchase that already happens.

What 1Heath Will Never Earn From

  • Payments to doctors of any kind, for prescribing, referring or ordering. The ethics code bars them[10].
  • Selling patient data, or using it for advertising.
  • Pharma advertising on the doctor's prescribing screen. Some Indian clinic software is partly paid for by drug companies through point-of-care advertising[4]; 1Heath will not do this.
  • Paid ranking of doctors for patients. The NMC's guidelines of 6 October 2026 bar it on platforms that list doctors[11].
  • Margin that depends on what the doctor prescribes. 1Heath earns from the pharmacy that buys, and the doctor's screen never ranks medicines by margin or by what 1Heath stocks.
  • Fees per lab test or per referral. Labs pay a flat fee, so 1Heath gains nothing from any one test.

How we will prove it, not just promise it:

  • Every fee is the same for every brand and every lab, and is shown to the clinic.
  • Labs and sellers are listed by distance, price and delivery time, never by what they pay 1Heath; the sorting rule is published.
  • Discounts and schemes go on the invoice to the licensed pharmacy business, never to a person and never on the doctor's screens.
  • Nothing on the doctor's screen changes because of a fee; a yearly outside review will check this and the result will be published.

Two outside reviews ranked these models independently. Both put a partner stockist first and 1Heath's own stock later, for the same reasons: no cash tied up, no batch liability, and the clinic can order big brands too. We agree, and keep 1Heath's own distribution as the destination.

At 1,000 doctors no setting covers a full team, servers, compliance and launch costs; that is normal for the first city. A 5% margin on everything loses money on every order, so 5% is only the floor for price-controlled medicines paid on delivery. Selling more barely helps while running costs take 10 of every 13 paise. What turns the business is scale, a margin nearer 16%, and an AI cost near ₹0.50 a visit (see the five-year path).

What the Pilot Must Measure Before Anyone Relies on This

  1. What a clinic pharmacy really orders each month, and what share it would move to the app.
  2. What price small makers will supply at, how much credit they really give after the first orders, and which of their medicines clinics will reorder.
  3. The fee per order a partner stockist accepts.
  4. The AI cost per visit on real consultations, in real languages.
  5. Whether doctors keep paying the membership after the free month, at which price from ₹199 to ₹499, and how much their pharmacy really saves.
  6. Whether labs pay a flat monthly fee, and whether patients book tests through 1Heath when they are free to choose any lab.
  7. The real running cost of supply: delivery, credit, expiry and the premises, as a share of sales.
