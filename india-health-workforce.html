Doctors, Nurses and Beds: Does India Have Enough?

How many doctors, nurses and hospital beds India has for every 10,000 people, how far it is from the norms, state by state and against eleven countries, why, and what India and the world can learn from each other.

Key Points
  • Picture 10,000 Indians. About 41 doctors, nurses and midwives are registered to care for them, but only about 13 are actually working in health. The World Health Organization's minimum is 44.5[1].
  • India has about 7 allopathic doctors and 17 nurses and midwives for every 10,000 people. The UK has 33 doctors and 95 nurses; Thailand has fewer doctors than India but twice the nurses[2].
  • Government hospitals have about 8.2 lakh beds[3]: about 6 beds for every 10,000 people. India's own minimum is 10, and its target for all beds, government and private, is 20[4].
  • The shortage is worst where people are poorest: Bihar has about 2 government beds for every 10,000 people, Kerala about 12[4]. Rural community health centres are missing 4 in 5 of the specialists they should have[5].
  • India trains doctors and nurses for the world. About 6.4 lakh Indian nurses work abroad[6], and India is a top source of foreign-born doctors in rich countries[7].

Picture 10,000 Indians

Ratios like "1 doctor per 1,000 people" are hard to feel. So take 10,000 Indians, a small town, and count who and what is there to look after them.

For every 10,000 IndiansAboutSource

Doctors, nurses and midwives actually working, by the national labour survey13Our working: about 19 lakh active workers[1] shared among about 146 crore people[8]

Government hospital beds6Our working: 8.2 to 8.6 lakh beds[3][9] among about 146 crore people

So, in a group of 100 Indians there is not even one health worker. On the registers there is about 1 for every 250 people; actually at work, about 1 for every 750. There is about one MBBS doctor for every 1,400 people. The government's own figure of "1 doctor for every 811 people" looks better because it adds AYUSH doctors and assumes 80% of everyone registered is working[10].

Registered is not the same as working. Only about a third of registered doctors, nurses and midwives are found working in the labour survey[1]. Some may have retired, moved abroad or left the profession, and some may appear on more than one state register. Every ratio on this page is better on paper than at the bedside.

Three Things the Table Shows

  • Doctors alone do not decide how long people live. Thailand has fewer doctors per person than India, but about twice the nurses, and its people live about four years longer (76.6 years against 72.2)[11]. See how health systems compare.
  • Rich countries choose very differently. Japan and South Korea have about 8 times India's beds; the UK and the US manage with about one and a half times. Beds matter less where more care happens in clinics and at home.
  • India's biggest gap is nurses. India has about 17 nurses and midwives for every 10,000 people. Brazil has about three times as many, and the UK more than five times[2].

Are There Enough Beds? How Many More Does India Need?

No. India's government hospitals had 8,18,661 beds as on 31 March 2023, the figure the Health Ministry gave the Lok Sabha[3]; the National Health Profile 2023 gives 8,60,688[9]. That covers primary and community health centres, sub-district and district hospitals and medical colleges. Private hospitals add more: estimates for all beds range from about 13[4] to 16[2] for every 10,000 people.

Here is how far India is from each yardstick. The "more beds needed" column is our own working, using about 146 crore people[8]; treat it as a rough size, not an exact count.

YardstickBeds per 10,000 peopleBeds India would needMore beds needed (our estimate)

The widely reported "India is short of 24 lakh beds"[4] matches the last row. The more useful target is the first: about 6 lakh more government beds. That would bring the national total up to the minimum India set for itself, and it is roughly three-quarters of what the country has built so far.

Beds are not care. A bed without a nurse, a doctor, oxygen and medicines is furniture. The specialist gaps below matter as much as the bed count.

State by State: Government Beds

Health is mainly a state responsibility under the Constitution, so the gap is really 36 different gaps. The bed counts below come from the Health Ministry's reply to Parliament[3]; the per-10,000 figures are as reported by The South First[4].

Small territories such as Lakshadweep, Puducherry and Chandigarh have more than 30 government beds for every 10,000 people[4]. The South First's national figure (0.79 per 1,000) does not match its own bed count and population, which work out to about 0.6, so treat the state ratios as approximate; the order of the states is the reliable part. An older Brookings analysis showed the same pattern, with Bihar lowest[12].

The five southern states hold about 29% of government beds; Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh together hold about 27%[3], though they are home to far more people. Bihar would need roughly four to five times its government beds, and Uttar Pradesh three times, to reach India's own minimum.

Why India Falls Short

  • The money is small. Government health spending was 1.43% of GDP in 2022–23[18]. The National Health Policy 2017 aimed for 2.5% by 2025, and that target was missed[19]. Families still pay about half of all health spending from their own pockets[18].
  • The people are in the wrong places. Villages have about 9 working health professionals for every 10,000 people, towns about 21. Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Jharkhand have fewer than 20 registered for every 10,000, while five southern and western states hold more than half of all registered professionals[1].
  • Government cannot fill specialist posts. Rural community health centres need 21,964 specialists and have 4,413: about 4 in 5 posts are empty. The gap is 83% for surgeons and 81% for paediatricians, and reaches 94% in Madhya Pradesh[5]. Even the AIIMS have 2,356 faculty posts vacant across 20 institutes; at AIIMS New Delhi, 446 of 1,306[20].
  • Most doctors work privately. Only about 13% of registered allopathic doctors work in government hospitals[1], where most poor patients go first.
  • Too few nurses, and many leave. India has about 17 nurses and midwives for every 10 doctors[1]; the UK has about 29[2]. About 6.4 lakh Indian nurses work abroad[6]. India is the second-largest source of foreign-born nurses in rich (OECD) countries, and one of the three largest for doctors[7].
  • Growth is recent. Medical colleges rose from 387 in 2014 to 818, and MBBS seats from 51,348 to 1,28,875[21]. A seat added today gives a specialist about ten years later (our estimate: about five and a half years of MBBS with internship, the entrance exam, then three years of specialist training).

What India Can Learn from Other Countries

CountryWhat they didThe lesson for India
ThailandSince 1974, new medical and nursing graduates must serve three years in rural areas. From 1995 it picked students from rural districts and trained them near home; the One District One Doctor track (2005) binds them to 12 years in their home town. After three years, about 80% of doctors from the rural track were still in district hospitals, against about 70% of regular graduates; after ten years, 16% against 10%. Everyone was covered by 2002[22].Choose students from the districts that need doctors, train them there, and bring them home. Bonds alone do not keep doctors; roots do.
CubaA family doctor-and-nurse team in every neighbourhood, each looking after up to 1,500 people, piloted in Havana in 1983 and later extended to the whole country[23]. Cuba has about 95 doctors for every 10,000 people[2].The first contact should be close to home and know the family. India's 1.78 lakh Ayushman Arogya Mandirs[16] are the start of this; they need staff who stay and records that follow the patient.
Thailand, Brazil, UKTwo to more than five times India's nurses for each person[2].Train, pay and trust nurses. A nurse can take on much of the screening, follow-up and chronic-disease care that now waits for a doctor.
United KingdomA family doctor (GP) as the first stop for most care. But 42% of doctors on the UK register qualified abroad, and about two-thirds of recent new joiners[24].A first stop that guides patients saves hospitals. Relying on other countries' doctors is fragile, and India is one of the countries the UK draws from.
Japan, South KoreaAbout 8 times India's hospital beds per person[2].Beds alone are not the goal; the UK and the US manage with far fewer. India should add government beds where the minimum is missed and put the rest into clinics and staff.

What Other Countries Can Learn from India

  • Community health workers at national scale. Over 10 lakh ASHA workers, all women, link villages to the health system for pregnancy care, vaccination, tuberculosis and nutrition. The WHO gave them its Global Health Leaders Award in 2022[25].
  • A doctor by video, for free. The government's eSanjeevani service had handled over 43 crore consultations by 23 November 2025[26].
  • Free hospital cover for crores of families. PM-JAY pays for hospital stays for the poorest 40% of families, and in most states for everyone aged 70 and over[27] (see government health schemes).
  • Training health workers for the world. Indian-trained doctors and nurses staff hospitals in many rich countries[7][6]. The lesson for those countries is to train enough of their own.

What Has to Change

  1. Fill the posts that exist. Specialist posts at community health centres and faculty posts at AIIMS are already sanctioned[5][20]. Pay, housing and career paths decide whether people take them.
  2. Add government beds where the minimum is missed, starting with Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, rather than evenly everywhere.
  3. Train far more nurses and let them do more, following Thailand and Brazil.
  4. Recruit locally and train locally, as Thailand did, so doctors stay in the districts that need them.
  5. Count properly. India has no current official count of private hospitals, and registers include people who are not working. Plans built on paper numbers will miss.
  6. Make each doctor's time go further. With about 7 MBBS doctors for every 10,000 people[2], time spent re-asking history or repeating tests is time taken from the next patient.

Where 1Heath fits. India cannot add specialists quickly. What can change now is how much of a doctor's short visit goes into finding out what happened before. That is the problem 1Heath works on.
