Design Principles

  • 1

    The doctor's attention is the scarcest resource. Every screen is judged by how few seconds and taps it costs in a two-minute visit.

  • 2

    Show what needs the doctor, hide what does not. Verified lines are quiet; only decisions stand out.

  • 3

    One way to do each thing. One edit button per section; no duplicated controls or hidden second paths.

  • 4

    The doctor's words are always visible. Every prescription line shows what was said beside what the app understood.

  • 5

    Nothing is chosen for the doctor. Uncertain names, strengths and durations appear as one-tap choices; none is pre-selected.

  • 6

    Familiar and readable. Material-style components any Android user knows; tested at 200% text size and on tablets; Indian languages first.

The Four Apps

Only the Doctor app is built today, as a working demo on an Android phone. The Desk, Pharmacy and Patient apps and the booking page are designed and exist only in the prototype.

What Crosses Between Apps, and What Never Does

The design starts from a privacy rule: each app sees only what its user needs.

From → toWhat crosses
Desk → DoctorToken, arrival time, vitals, consent. Never a note.
Doctor → DeskQueue position and session state only
Doctor → PharmacyThe signed prescription: drug, dose, duration, prescriber
Pharmacy → DoctorA substitution request for a non-scheduled drug; stock counts
Doctor ↔ PatientThe consented record, signed notes and the patient's copy
Booking → DeskThe booked token, which joins the same queue
  • ✕

    Clinical notes never reach the desk or the pharmacy. A patient's history is seen only by the treating doctor and the patient; the desk and the pharmacy see only today's task.

  • ✕

    The pharmacy sees the prescription, never the consultation.

  • ✕

    A clinic administrator manages the clinic and never reads a record.

Where the prototype and the current build differ, the build wins. For example, the prototype imagines the note never leaving the doctor's phone; the current build drafts the note with an India-hosted cloud model and verifies it on the phone, as described on the architecture page.

The Review Screen, in Detail

Rules the Screens Follow

  • A food timing that came from the catalogue, not from speech, is shown in grey as a default the doctor can change.
  • A missing duration shows one-tap choices: 3 days, 5 days, 7 days, 1 month, continue.
  • An uncertain name shows up to three candidates with their composition; look-alike pairs always appear together.
  • A when-needed medicine without a condition shows: if fever, if pain, if needed.
  • Status colours mean the same thing in every app: green verified, amber confirm, red cannot sign.

The Prescription PDF

Designed to look like a real Indian prescription and to include what the rules ask for, as we read them (our reading; to be confirmed by a lawyer): the doctor's name, recognised qualifications and registration number; patient name, age, sex, address, date and weight for children, with allergies shown prominently; vitals, complaints and diagnosis; each medicine with brand, generic in capitals, dosing in words, food timing, duration and total quantity, plus a line in the patient's language; advice, follow-up date and signature. Four sample sheets are designed: adult, child, Ayurveda and homeopathy.

It is not printed by default. The signed prescription reaches the pharmacy through the Pharmacy app, and the patient through the Patient app or a WhatsApp link. The Doctor app demo already makes the PDF; the sending is designed, not built.
